# Effect of Surface Oxygen Groups and Water on Hydrogen Spillover in Pt-Doped Activated Carbon

## Qixiu Li<sup>†</sup> and Angela D. Lueking\*<sup>,†,‡</sup>

†

Department of Energy & Mineral Engineering, The Pennsylvania State University, University Park, Pennsylvania 16802, United States

‡

Department of Chemical Engineering, & EMS Energy Institute, The Pennsylvania State University, University Park, Pennsylvania 16802, United States

bS Supporting Information

ABSTRACT: The synergistic role of increased oxygen functional

groups and water on hydrogen adsorption via hydrogen spillover is

explored. A potassium hydroxide (KOH) treatment is used to increase

the surface density of oxygen groups on a high surface area-activated

carbon (AC), which serves as a secondary receptor for hydrogen spill-

over after physical mixing with a common Pt-based spillover catalyst.

Consistent with previous results, XPS shows that KOH treatment

increases atomic concentration of oxygen and the fraction of carbonyl/

quinone groups on the surface of the AC. Increased surface density of

oxygen groups leads to a significant enhancement of hydrogen spillover

at pressures <100 mbar. At 300 K, the hydrogen uptake is 1.1 wt % at

100 mbar and increases to 1.4 wt % at 20 bar. However, only 0.4 wt % of

this is desorbable via a pressure reduction at room temperature, and

appreciable spillover is observed only when trace water is present during pretreatment. The trace water is believed to affect the development of active surface sites based on characterization of the development of oxygen groups by XPS and FTIR. The synergistic role of oxygen groups and water and a plausible mechanism on the effect on hydrogen isotherms are discussed.

1. INTRODUCTION

Hydrogen spillover, which has been observed in a variety of catalytic reactions, such as hydrogenation,<sup>1,2</sup> syngas reactions,<sup>3,4</sup> and hydrotreatment reaction,<sup>5</sup> among others, is a well-known phenomenon in catalysis. The role of spillover was recognized in catalyst’s longevity and stability of activity and selectivity,<sup>6</sup> creation of catalytic centers,<sup>7</sup> and enhancement of reaction rate.<sup>8</sup> Comprehensive reviews of spillover in catalysis have been published,<sup>6,9-11</sup> including those specific to hydrogen storage.<sup>12,13</sup> Hydrogen spillover was applied in hydrogen storage in recent years by Lueking and Yang,<sup>14</sup> and appreciable enhance- ment of hydrogen storage by hydrogen spillover has been achieved by the following work.<sup>15-18</sup> Hydrogen spillover is a process composed of multiple steps, including catalyzed hydro- gen dissociation, surface diffusion from the catalyst to a surface that would otherwise be unable to adsorb dissociated hydrogen directly, possible chemisorption of the dissociated hydrogen species to the support, and recombination and subsequent desorption of mobile hydrogen species from the support. (The generic term “species” is used, as the exact nature of spilt-over hydrogen is unclear in the literature, with H<sub>3</sub> , H<sup>þ</sup> ions, H atoms, and coexistence of H<sup>þ</sup> and H atom possible depending upon the nature of the surface.<sup>19</sup>) The balance of these competing processes determines the overall hydrogen content on a surface. As such, hydrogen spillover differs from simple physisorption or

chemisorption for which there is direct equilibration between the bulk gas phase and the active adsorption sites. Although hydro- gen spillover includes both chemisorption and physisorption as steps in its mechanistic sequence, the process of surface diffusion effectively decouples the interaction between the gas phase and the active surface sites. This decoupling leads to dependence on structural features that affect hydrogen supply to the surface (e.g., catalyst size, dispersion, interface, diffusion pathway) versus the probability of chemisorption (number of active surface sites; binding energy with hydrogen) versus the probability of recom- bination. The premise in utilizing hydrogen spillover to enhance overall hydrogen uptake is that either the population of mobile hydrogen species is appreciable with continual resupply from the catalyst, or the dissociated active hydrogen species migrate to and are retained by surface sites to which they weakly chemisorb. To improve overall uptake via hydrogen spillover, research has included investigation of interfacial contact between metal catalyst and support,<sup>15,16</sup> metal doping efficiencies,<sup>20,21</sup> structure of the support,<sup>22,23</sup> substitutional doping of the support with heteroatoms,<sup>24-27</sup> or altering of the surface chemistry of the

In catalysis, the reduction of a large number of metal oxides has been improved by hydrogen spillover.<sup>28</sup> In particular, tung- sten and molybdenum oxide hydrogen bronzes have been thoroughly studied since Khoobiar’s initial experiments.<sup>29</sup> It is also well documented that surface oxygen groups, specifically terminal hydroxyls, can facilitate hydrogen spillover to zeolites,<sup>30</sup> silica, and alumina.<sup>31-33</sup> The oxygen groups act to either stabilize the spilt-over hydrogen<sup>30,34</sup> or provide bridges by which H atoms can migrate over the support surface to occupy active sites previously inaccessible to them.<sup>33</sup> However, the desorption of spilt-over hydrogen may occur via dehydroxylation of the sup- port in such cases.<sup>30</sup> There is significantly less data on how surface functional groups on carbon-based supports will affect hydrogen spillover. Wang et al. modified a carbon receptor with oxygen groups and found that the presence of oxygen groups increased the binding energies of the spilt-over H on graphite.<sup>35</sup> Theoretical calculations showed increased hydrogen binding energy upon the oxidation of the carbon surface<sup>13</sup> and possible routes for decreasing diffusion barriers.<sup>36</sup> The first objective of this study is to report the effect of added oxygen groups on hydrogen spillover, with emphasis on investigation of the chem- ical identity of specific surface oxygen functional groups. To this end, we use a modified alkaline treatment to increase the number of oxygen functional groups on the surface and use multiple characterization techniques to understand the nature of the oxygen species active in accepting spilt-over hydrogen. The low-temperature alkaline treatment, modified from Chiang et al.,<sup>37</sup> is expected to increase hydroxyl, carboxyl, and carbonyl groups on the surface of an activated carbon. This work is a follow-up from a published conference proceeding from 2008.<sup>38</sup> In preparation of this work and verification of the magnitude of these results, we have found interesting effects of trace water present during pretreatment. The role of water, as will be discussed, lies in surface modification during pretreatment, rather than (or in addition to) directly affecting adsorption. Although water is generally considered to be a detrimental contaminant to hydrogen storage,<sup>39,40</sup> water is also known as a necessary cocatalyst to activate hydrogen spillover,<sup>41-43</sup> and its presence during pretreatment and/or adsorption<sup>11,33</sup> may play a role in subsequent hydrogen spillover uptake in a process akin to the bridge-building process developed by Yang et al.<sup>15,16</sup> The second objective of the article is thus to explore the role of water, and its synergistic role in the development of surface chemistry and subsequent effect on hydrogen storage via the spillover phenomenon.

## 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

2.1. Material Synthesis. A high surface area activated carbon (AC, The Kansai Coke & Chemicals) was selected for additional surface modification. This AC was originally prepared by alkali- metal activation of a graphitized petroleum coke. To increase surface oxygen groups further, we treated the as-received AC with KOH, drawing from a procedure reported by Chiang et al.<sup>37</sup> The procedure was modified to use KOH rather than NaOH because KOH is widely used in the modification of carbon material for hydrogen storage.<sup>22,44</sup> In brief, the AC was impreg- nated with 1 M potassium hydroxide (KOH, EMD Chemicals) in a KOH to AC weight ratio of 4:1, magnetically stirred for 12 h at room temperature, washed to neutral, filtered, then dried at 373 K. The resulting material after KOH activation will be referred to as AC-o.

The presence of oxygen groups is expected to affect the dispersion of the Pt catalyst, and thus secondary spillover studies were used to emphasize the role of surface chemistry without altering the “supply” of the hydrogen species from the catalyst. The secondary carbon support (either AC or AC-o) was mixed with a Pt catalyst supported on activated carbon (5% Pt-C, STEM Chemicals) in a ratio of Pt-C to AC fixed at 1:9,<sup>15</sup> then ground with an agate mortar and pestle for 30 min (Pt-C/AC or Pt-C/AC-o). In each series of experiments described below, samples within the series are from the same batch of synthesis.

2.2. Characterization. BET specific surface area and total pore volume were calculated from nitrogen adsorption/desorp- tion isotherms at 77 K with Micromeritics ASAP 2020. The micropore volumes were calculated from CO<sub>2</sub> isotherms using the Dubinin-Astakhov (DA) equation. Before both N<sub>2</sub> and CO<sub>2</sub> isotherms, the samples were outgassed at 2 mmHg for 6 h at 423 K.

The chemical bonding information of the modified surface was obtained by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Axis Ultra; Kratos). The X-ray source for the measurement was monochromatic Al-KR. The working pressure was maintained under 10<sup>-8</sup> Torr. 80 eV pass energy was used for surveys, and 20 eV was for high-resolution scans. For calibration purpose, the C (1s) electron bond energy was referred at 284.6 eV, which is corresponding to graphitic carbon.<sup>45</sup> O-to-C atomic ratios were approximated from the XPS survey spectra by using the ratio of areas under the most intense photoelectron peak of each element. Because the spot diameter of XPS is in millimeter scale, typically single spots were used. Two different preparations of AC and AC-o were used (as noted below) in the quantification of the O/C ratios to demonstrate sample variability. Samples were exposed to air prior to characterization. We make the assumption that air exposure during sample transfer is expected to have minimal effect on the surface groups that remain chemically bound to the surface and could not be desorbed; our data show similar results regardless of time between synthesis/adsorption and characterization.

Diffuse reflectance infrared spectra (DRIFTS) were collected on a Magna-IR 750 Nicolet FTIR spectrometer in the range of 500-4000 cm<sup>-1</sup>. All spectra were collected at 50 °C. Samples were exposed to air prior to characterization.

## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1. Carbon Oxidation via KOH Treatment and Setup I Results. 3.1.1. Effect of Carbon Oxidation on Surface Chemistry and Physisorption. The textural properties of AC and AC-o (Table 1), indicate that the KOH activation process decreases the BET surface area and pore volume (total and micro-) while decreasing the average pore diameter. The properties are con- sistent with the idea that oxygen groups may block access of N<sub>2</sub> to some pores at 77 K. The XPS survey spectra of both AC and AC-o (Figure 2) exhibit the O 1s and C 1s peaks; K 2s peak is observed in AC-o, a sign of residue from the KOH treatment. Quantification of the XPS survey spectra (Table 2) shows that the KOH-activation increases the atomic concentration of O on the surface: O-to-C atomic ratio increased from 0.23 for AC to 0.28 for AC-o. (In a separate preparation of AC and AC-o, the O to C atomic ratio increased from 0.19 for AC to 0.28 for AC-o. The increase in oxygen content is ∼32%, which is close to the data presented in the paper from which the KOH oxidation technique is based.<sup>37</sup>) High-resolution C 1s (Figure 3) and O 1s XPS spectra (Figure 4) show that the increase in the surface O groups in AC-o are either carbonyl or quinone groups (peak II in Figure 3; peak I in Figure 4). KOH treatment has thus increased the number of oxygen functional groups and increased the relative proportion of carbonyl or quinone groups. As stated above, the KOH treatment is an adaptation of a method reported by Chiang et al.<sup>37</sup> that used NaOH treatment. Chiang et al. reported that NaOH treatment added hydroxyls, carboxylic acids, and lesser amounts of carbonyl groups, citing primarily Boehm titration, shifts in IR spectra, and electophoretic mobility. Because the characterization methods differ, it is difficult to compare directly the results of the alkaline treatment here with the findings of Chiang et al.

3.3. Evolution of Oxygen Surface Functional Groups. 3.3.1. Effect of KOH Oxidation on Carbon Surface

Chemistry. To reiterate, KOH treatment increases the surface content of oxygen functional groups (Table 2), and the relative fraction of carbonyl or quinone-type groups is increased. Ulti- mately, the oxidation treatment leads to high low-pressure hydrogen spillover during adsorption. After the H<sub>2</sub> adsorp- tion-desorption cycle, XPS and FTIR both suggest that the nondesorbable H<sub>2</sub> (1 wt % for Pt-C/AC-o in Setup 1; 0.59 wt % for wPt-C/AC-o in Setup 2) is in the form of hydroxyls. We note that carbonyl groups may be hydrogenated to form hydroxyl groups.<sup>58</sup> Characterization postadsorption would obviously not probe the nature of the readily desorbed hydrogen present for spillover samples (e.g., ∼0.25 wt % for Pt-C/AC-o in Setup 1), and this readily desorbed fraction cannot be attributed to chemisorption or physisorption. By the process of deduction and considering theoretically predicted binding energies, we speculate that spillover hydrogen loosely bound to carbon sites (previously referred to as “riding modes”<sup>59</sup>) contributes to this 0.25 wt % desorbable H<sub>2</sub> .

3.3.2. Effect of Trace Water during Pretreatment. The pro- nounced low-pressure hydrogen uptake is observed for oxidized samples only when trace water is found in pretreatment (i.e., Setup 1 and wPt-C/AC-o in Setup 2). However, the behavior cannot be attributed to the presence of water during adsorption (Figure 6I). The ex situ characterization methods complicate identification of the surface functionalities directly after pretreatment. However, indirect evidence of the effect of surface chemistry can be induced from comparison of the as-synthesized to the dry or wet pretreated samples after hydro- gen adsorption. Three effects will be discussed: (1) the introduc- tion of O groups during pretreatment; (2) the role of water in dehydration of other groups; and (3) the possible reaction between H<sub>2</sub> O and carbon. First, the O/C ratio for dPt-C/AC-o is relatively stable between preparation and postsorption char- acterization (Table 2). In contrast, the O/C ratio increases for wPt-C/AC-o, suggesting that the presence of water has added oxygen groups. In both dry and wet pretreatments, the nature of the oxygen groups has changed, as reflected by an increase in Peak II oxygen on the surface (O atoms in esters, carboxy- lic anhydrides and oxygen atoms in hydroxyls or ethers<sup>60,61</sup> ) after sorption (Figure 7). This increase indicates introduction of these groups during pretreatment (particularly for “wet” with its increased O/C), conversion of Peak I oxygen groups (carbonyl/quinones) to Peak II groups, or both. In general, the number of oxygen groups on a carbon surface is expected to decrease with heating in an inert gas,<sup>52,62</sup> and carboxyl groups (-COO) correlated to CO<sub>2</sub> evolution are expected to evolve at temperatures as low as 150 °C.<sup>52</sup> However, the evolution/modification of surface-functional groups is more complicated when treated in H<sub>2</sub> . For example, Kundu et al.<sup>62</sup> analyzed the evolution of gaseous species when oxidized carbon nano- tubes are treated in H<sub>2</sub> and note the possibility for dehydration of COOH and COH between 150 and 400 °C; reactive carbon edge sites remaining after dehydration would be expected to form C- H in the presence of H<sub>2</sub>. One might also suspect direct reduction of oxygen groups in hydrogen to form water. However, in our case, water evolution is not observed during pretreatment (Supporting Information). Rather, a decreasing water MS signal during the wet pretreatment (relative to the dry pretreatment) suggests that water might participate in a reaction on the carbon surface (Figure SI-4 in the Supporting Information). 3.4. Role of Oxygen Groups in Hydrogen Spillover: Pro- posed Mechanisms. We show that increased oxygen groups, particularly of carbonyl/quinone type, correlate with increased low-pressure hydrogen spillover. Several experimental<sup>35,38</sup> and theoretical<sup>36</sup> studies that have attempted to increase the number of oxygen groups on spillover materials reported that adding surface oxygen can improve hydrogen spillover and stabilize

19,35,63,64

spilt-over hydrogen.	In particular, a recent paper by Yang et al. showed that the oxygen groups, especially semiqui- none group, increased the reversible hydrogen storage capacity significantly.<sup>65</sup> However, the role of oxygen groups in hydrogen spillover needs further study; explanations for this effect can be categorized as follows: (I) bridge-building, (II) surface binding and chemical kinetics, and (III) oxidation and reduction. In terms of (I), surface oxygen groups may provide diffusion paths, in a method analogous to a “bridging”<sup>42</sup> or a “brigade”.<sup>11</sup> Theoreti- cally, adjacent oxygen groups increase binding energies between hydrogen and carbon surfaces<sup>35</sup> as well as bind directly to semiquinone or lactone groups.<sup>65</sup> Hydroxyl groups or ethers may lower the energy path for diffusion.<sup>36</sup> In terms of (II), Lueking et al.<sup>46</sup> showed that increased binding energy leads to increased surface coverage using a Monte Carlo analysis of the kinetics of this spillover reaction sequence assuming a “loosely bound” hydrogen spillover state, as demonstrated experimentally by Mitchell et al. via inelastic neutron scattering.<sup>59</sup> Hydrogen species bound to the surface with sufficient energy could account for the ∼1 wt % residual chemisorbed hydrogen found as surface hydroxyls in postadsorption XPS characterization. In terms of (III), oxidation (either the KOH treatment or the subsequent “wet” pretreatment) is expected to modify the electronic proper- ties of the surface. Oxidation-reduction reactions on carbon

surfaces have been reviewed,<sup>66</sup> but the process is complicated by not only the type of carbon and oxygen groups but also their proximity and ability to stabilize any introduced charges. More characterization is needed to verify this idea.

3.5. Optimization of Surface Functional Groups for Spil- lover. Here we characterize but do not necessarily control or adapt synthesis to give preferential formation to a particular type of oxygen group. Yang et al. recently suggested that semiquinone groups are the ideal receptor of spilt-over hydrogen because lactone groups will lead to irreversible hydrogen adsorption because of stronger binding energy.<sup>65</sup> Psofogiannakis et al’s<sup>36</sup> simulation showed that desorption of spilt-over hydrogen bound to oxygen functional groups desorbs more favorably as water rather than molecular hydrogen. If true, this would have obvious negative consequences for the reversibility of hydrogen storage in oxygen-modified carbon materials. Whether the hydrogen that remains chemically bound to the surface after pressure reduction to 12 mbar will desorb as H<sub>2</sub> or H<sub>2</sub>O will be the subject of a subsequent paper. We anticipate that this will depend on the nature of the oxygen functional groups, and different oxidation methods may bring about different oxygen groups. Further experimental work includes temperature-programmed desorp- tion experiments on various oxygen functional groups to identify desorbed species and check the formation of water on the surface. For now, the increase in the amount of readily desorbable hydrogen with oxidation (0.25 wt % for Pt-C/AC-o vs 0.06% for Pt-C/AC) seems to support hypothesis II.

## 4. CONCLUSIONS

In the current study, XPS characterization results show that KOH treatment produces oxygen groups on an activated carbon sample with a large fraction being quinone type groups. The oxidation treatment has a negligible effect on physisorption but leads to a significant enhancement of hydrogen adsorption in the presence of a hydrogen-dissociation catalyst, that is, hydrogen spillover. Gravimetric adsorption measurements show that hy- drogen spillover to the oxidized surface is pronounced at low pressures (P < 100 mbar), with an H/M<sub>T</sub> ratio of 444 and a seven-fold increase in overall hydrogen uptake relative to its constituents. The presence of water during pretreatment is necessary to realize this low-pressure enhancement, and signifi- cant hydrogen spillover was not observed when scientific grade hydrogen was used during pretreatment. We speculate that this is due to increased oxygen functional groups formed during pre- treatment in the presence of water, active carbon sites formed via water reaction on the carbon surface, or both. The mechanism for the water-assisted pretreatment needs further clarification, as does its alignment with several proposed mechanisms on the effect of surface chemistry on hydrogen spillover. The pro- nounced low-pressure effect agrees with other publications<sup>50,67</sup> but is expected to be highly dependent on the nature of the sample. The current results indicate that oxidation of the surface of the support will facilitate low-pressure, ambient-temperature hydrogen spillover but may not necessarily lead to hydrogen that readily desorbs at ambient temperature. XPS and FTIR surface characterization after H<sub>2</sub> desorption suggests that strongly bound hydrogen forms hydroxyls with introduced functional groups; there is only a vague indication of new carbon-hydrogen bonds. A certain fraction of hydrogen is found to be loosely bound in that it readily desorbs with pressure reduction and cannot be accounted for by physisorption to the carbon materials.

However, the state of this loosely bound hydrogen cannot be determined without in situ methods. On-going work is looking at the effect of surface sites on the temperature of desorption and on the nature of the desorbed species. The lack of hydrogen desorption without heating leads to practical engineering hurdles for hydrogen storage and also highlights that both adsorption and desorption isotherms should be considered in the evaluation of modified carbon materials. The results also show that the oxidation of carbon species to facilitate hydrogen spillover is dependent on both the amount and the nature of surface oxygen species.
