ABSTRACT: In this work dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs) based on Zn<sub>2</sub> SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were fabricated for the first time. High yield growth of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were achieved through the optimization of the growth conditions. The adsorption of the N719 dye on Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires and its effect on cell performance were inspected. From a comparison study of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowire- and nanoparticle-DSSCs, a more than 0.1 V improvement on open-circuit voltage was observed when Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were used as the photoanode. The relation between open-circuit voltage and back electron transfer reaction was examined. Due to the advantages of

ternary metal oxides and single crystal nanowires, such nanostructures could lead to a more versatile selection for photoanode materials and improved performance of sensitized solar cells.

1. INTRODUCTION

There has been significant research interest in dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs) since the breakthrough work of O’Regan

and Gratzel in 1991,<sup>1</sup> and most recently DSSCs with efficiency

as high as 12.7% have been achieved.<sup>2</sup> The application of nanostructured materials such as nanoparticles and nanowires

in DSSCs could lower the fabrication cost, create flexible structures, and provide possibilities to improve light absorption

and carrier collection in the devices. Thus far the majority of published works has been focused on photoanodes based on

1,3−5 6−8	9−11

binary metal oxides such as TiO <sub>2</sub>, ZnO, and SnO<sub>2</sub>.

Among them, TiO<sub>2</sub> is the most popular and widely studied one

due to its high power conversion efficiency and low preparation cost.<sup>1,12</sup> However, research has also shown that TiO<sub>2</sub> photoanodes could cause photobleaching of the dye molecules

in the UV region of the solar spectrum,<sup>13,14</sup> highlighting the importance of exploring other promising materials for DSSC applications. Compared with simple binary materials, ternary metal oxides such as Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO <sub>4</sub>, Zn<sub>2</sub>TiO<sub>4</sub>, SrTiO<sub>3</sub>, etc. offer several advantages on certain aspects.<sup>15−17</sup> First, ternary metal oxides provide a broader material spectrum for sensitized solar

cell applications. Second, by altering the compositions of the ternary metal oxides, their chemical and electrical properties

can be easily modified. A well-known example is ITOby adjusting the In:Sn ratio, the material’s electrical conductivity, optical transmission, and surface work function can be easily changed.<sup>18</sup> The ability to fine-tune these properties is crucial for realizing a better control of carrier transport and collection in sensitized solar structures.<sup>19</sup> In addition, ternary metal oxides

also exhibit better corrosion resistance than many binary oxides,

which could improve the long-term cell stability under the real world conditions.<sup>15</sup>

In this work we investigated the synthesis of Zn <sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires and present for the first time the results of dye-sensitized solar cells fabricated using these nanowires. Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> is a ternary metal oxide with a wide band gap (∼3.6 eV)<sup>20</sup> and high electron mobility (10−15 cm<sup>3</sup> V<sup>−1</sup> s<sup>−1</sup>).<sup>21</sup> It is also an earth-abundant material, which makes it suitable for the fabrication of low-cost DSSCs.<sup>18</sup> Nanostructures based on Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> have been reported previously;<sup>22−25</sup> however, to the best of our knowledge, so far only Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanoparticles have been utilized as photoanodes in DSSCs.<sup>15,22</sup> Compared with nanowires, nanoparticles have larger surface area for dye loading, and the overall power conversion efficiencies of nanoparticle-DSSCs are usually higher than those of nanowire- DSSCs. Nonetheless, nanowires still present several attractive features as photoanodes in DSSCs. For example, nanowires provide direct transport pathway for photogenerated electrons and hence can result in enhanced charge collection efficiency in the devices.<sup>6</sup> Due to the improved diffusion length, longer wires and thus thicker nanowire films can be incorporated into DSSCs, which can potentially improve the quantum efficiency in the long-wavelength region of the solar spectrum.<sup>26</sup> In addition, the reduced surface area and fast transport in nanowires could also suppress the back electron transfer reaction that occurs at the photoanode/redox electrolyte solution interface and lead to a higher open-circuit voltage (V<sub>OC</sub>).<sup>27</sup> In this research, Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were synthesized and different growth conditions were inspected in order to eliminate unwanted phases and achieve the best yield of

## 2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Nanowire Fabrication. Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were synthe- sized on silicon substrates by chemical vapor deposition through the vapor−liquid−solid (VLS) growth mechanism. A gold film with a thickness of 3 nm was first deposited on a silicon substrate by thermal evaporation as the catalyst. Source material that consists of Zn foil (Alfa Aesar, 0.25 mm thick, 99.98%) and SnO powder (Alfa Aesar, 99.9%) was loaded into an alumina boat. The alumina boat was placed at the center of a 1 in. single zone tube furnace, and the gold-coated silicon substrate was positioned at a distance of 5 cm downstream from the source boat. The tube furnace was evacuated to 10 mtorr and purged by dry N<sub>2</sub> gas three times. The temperature of tube furnace was then raised to 900 °C with a ramping rate of 30 °C/min. Upon elevating the furnace temperature, a 100 sccm N<sub>2</sub> gas flow was introduced into the tube furnace, and the pressure was kept at 10 torr throughout the whole growth process. An O <sub>2</sub> flow was also injected into the tube at a certain temperature during the ramping process. The effects of the source material ratio, O <sub>2</sub> flow rate, and O<sub>2</sub> injection temperature on Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowire growth were inspected, and an optimized growth condition was realized, which could repeatedly produce single crystalline Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires with a high yield.

Structural Characterization. The crystalline structure of the Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Philips X’Pert MPD diffractometer and a Cu K<sub>α</sub> source. The morphology was studied by a FEI Quanta 450FEG field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) examination was conducted with a FEI Tecnai F20-UT microscope. The simulation of high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images was conducted by using a multislice code, National Center for Electron Microscopy Simulation Software (NCEMSS).

DSSCs Fabrication. The fabrication of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowire- DSSCs started with a printing transfer process of the nanowires from the silicon substrate to F:SnO <sub>2</sub> (FTO) coated transparent substrate (MTI Corporation, 2.5 mm thick, 12−14 Ω/sq, 82−84.5% visible transmittance). The FTO substrate was first spin- coated with a 100 nm thick Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> precursor film [0.16 M tin 2-ethyhexahoate (Alfa Aesar, 96%), 0.33 M zinc acetate (Alfa Aesar, 98%), and 0.5 M triethanolamine (Alfa Aesar, 98%) in ethanol]. This Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> precursor film served as an adhesion layer between the transferred nanowires and the FTO substrate and could improve the electrical contact between the two after annealing. In a typical printing transfer process, Zn <sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires on a silicon substrate were faced down and pressed to the precursor-coated FTO substrate under a pressure of 1000 g/cm<sup>2</sup>, which was maintained for 1 min before release. This approach could reproducibly create nanowire thin films with a thickness of about 20 μm. The FTO substrate with nanowires was then annealed at 500 °C for 5 h to transform the precursor into a Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO <sub>4</sub> film. To sensitize the Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub>

nanowires, a 0.05 mM (Bu <sub>4</sub>N)<sub>2</sub>Ru(dcbpyH)<sub>2</sub>(NCS)<sub>2</sub> (N719 dye) in dry ethanol solution was used, and the sensitization time ranged from 15 min to 24 h. After dye loading, the nanowires were rinsed in ethanol and blow dried by N<sub>2</sub> . Another FTO substrate coated with 20 nm Pt was used as the counter electrode and was bonded together with the nanowire/ FTO substrate through a hot-melt spacer (Bynel, Dupont, 75 μm). Liquid electrolyte (50 mM I<sub>2</sub>, 0.5 M LiI, and 0.5 M 4-terbutylpyridine in 3-methoxypropionitrile) was injected into the capsulated space through predrilled holes. Characterization of device performance was carried out immediately after the fabrication. The synthesis of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanoparticles and fabrication of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanoparticle-DSSCs followed previ- ously reported procedures.<sup>15,22</sup> The thickness of the Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanoparticle film was controlled to be 20 μm, the same as the Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowire film.

J−V, IPCE, and EIS Measurements. Current density− voltage (J−V) curves were acquired by a source-measurement unit in the dark and under a simulated sunlight (100 mW/cm<sup>2</sup>, calibrated by a KG-5 filtered silicon photodiode) using a setup with a xenon lamp. Incident-photon-to-current conversion efficiency (IPCE) data were obtained with a monochromatic light source that consists of a 50 W tungsten halogen lamp and a monochromator. The output of the monochromator was modulated by a chopper, and the photocurrent was measured using a lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research SR830). Two- electrode mode was used to obtain the Tafel plots of the DSSCs. EIS measurement was performed using an Ivium CompactStat potentiostat. The measurement followed the procedure described in ref 28, and the spectra were obtained at a bias of −0.6 V with 10 mV modulation from 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz. All measurements were carried out in the dark.

## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Research efforts have been previously made to fabricate Zn <sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires by chemical vapor deposition using different source materials.<sup>24,25,29</sup> However, these methods generally require strict parameter windows that are difficult to reproduce. Unlike the growth of binary oxide nanowires, introducing a third element into the synthesis of ternary nanostructures may result in multiple phases in the final products. For example, in the VLS growth of Zn<sub>2</sub> SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires, other phases including ZnO and SnO <sub>2</sub> may also present. The ZnO phase can be easily removed by acid etching due to the instability of ZnO in acidic solutions.<sup>30</sup> However, it is very difficult to eliminate the SnO<sub>2</sub> phase using similar chemical methods. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that the conduction band of SnO<sub>2</sub> is lower than those of conventional solar cell anode materials (TiO <sub>2</sub> or ZnO), which causes a higher recombination rate of the injected electrons with the electrolytes.<sup>10,11</sup> Therefore, proper synthesis con- ditions should be developed to avoid the growth of the SnO<sub>2</sub> phase in Zn<sub>2</sub> SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowire fabrication. In this research, a series of experiments were carried out to investigate the effects of the ratio of source material, oxygen flow rate, and oxygen injection temperature on the phases of the fabricated nanowires. Figure 1a shows the XRD characterization data of the nanowires synthesized with different Zn:Sn ratio but same oxygen flow rate (3 sccm) and oxygen injection temperature (800 °C). For the Zn-deficient growth (Zn:Sn = 1:1), only a small amount of nanowires including Zn <sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> as well as unwanted phases such as SnO<sub>2</sub> and Sn were obtained, which made the purification process very difficult. Similar studies were also carried out for oxygen flow rate and injection temperature, and the optimized growth parameters for Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were determined to be Zn:Sn ratio of 3:1, oxygen flow rate of 3 sccm, and oxygen injection temperature of 800 °C. Zn<sub>2</sub> SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires could be identified by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis. Similar to previous reports,<sup>29,31</sup> both axially periodic and smooth Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were obtained in our fabrication. The lengths of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were longer than 200 μm, and the diameters ranged from 80 to 150 nm.

The crystalline structure of the axially periodic Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires was characterized by TEM. Figure 2a shows a cubic unit cell of the spinel AB<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub> structure. Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> has an inverted-spinel structure, with 50% of the Zn ions occupying the A sites and the other 50% occupying the B sites together with the Sn ions. The basic repetition unit of an axially periodic Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowire was a rhombohedral single crystal enclosed by {111} equivalent facets, which is depicted in Figure 2b. A TEM image of a Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowire with periodic structure is shown in Figure 2c. The Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowire growth direction was identified as [11 1] by a selected area electron diffraction (SAED) study. Figure 2d is a HRTEM image of a portion of the Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowire shown in Figure 2c, which is indicated by a square. The image was captured along the [011] zone axis, and the lattice constants along the [11 1] and [200] directions are specified in the image. The inset in Figure 2d is the corresponding fast-Fourier-transform (FFT) image, revealing the single crystalline structure and the [111] growth direction. It has been previously reported that axially periodic Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires can have either single crystalline or segmented structures that are connected by twin grain boundaries.<sup>24,29</sup> However, in our fabrication only single crystalline Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were obtained, and they did not exhibit either twin boundary or stacking fault defects. Simulation of the HRTEM image was conducted using the National Center for Electron Microscopy Simulation Software (NCEMSS) with an objective spherical aberration (C<sub>S</sub>) of 0.52 mm. Figure 2e is the enlarged TEM image, and Figure 2f is the simulated image (sample thickness, 40 nm; defocus, −57 nm). The A and B ion sites of an AB<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub> unit cell that produce the TEM pattern were overlaid on the corresponding portion of the simulated image in Figure 2f. The simulation result matched the TEM image, confirming the inverted-spinel structure of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires. It is also important to point out that the perovskite ZnSnO<sub>3</sub> phase was not presented in our synthesis. This phase has only been observed in vapor−solid<sup>32</sup> or high-pressure solution-based growth.<sup>33</sup>

## 4. CONCLUSIONS

In this study we carried out the synthesis of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires and fabricated DSSCs based on these nanostruc- tures. Optimized conditions for a reproducible synthesis of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were achieved, and the effect of dye adsorption on cell performance was investigated. A more than 0.1 V improvement on open-circuit voltage was constantly observed when Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowires were used as the photoanode instead of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanoparticles. The higher open-circuit voltage of Zn<sub>2</sub>SnO<sub>4</sub> nanowire-DSSCs could be attributed to a suppressed back electron transfer process in the devices. A key factor that limits the performance of nanowire-DSSCs is the smaller surface area for dye loading. However, as our research has shown, this disadvantage could in fact result in an improved open-circuit voltage, which could benefit device application under certain special conditions. Nanowire-based photoanodes could also provide an important test platform for the investigation of selected fundamental issues in sensitized solar cells, which could generate additional insight on the understanding of carrier generation and collection processes in such structures.
