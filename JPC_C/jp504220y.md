Recently, considerable attention has been paid to the “strain effect” in which crystal distortion changes electronic charge carrier and/or mass transport properties.<sup>1−6</sup> The electronic effects of induced strain in a crystal lattice are highly interesting, because these effects may enable performance improvements in electrochemical solid state devices such as solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs). There have been several reports on the positive effects of strain on electrical conductivity and oxygen diffusivity.<sup>7−20</sup> Most approaches employ epitaxial thin film growth, and strain is induced by a crystal lattice mismatch between the substrate and the film. However, for thin films the quality of the film such

K<sub>2</sub>NiF<sub>4</sub> structure and has an extremely high oxygen permeability, as was previously reported.<sup>23−28</sup> In this study, the effect of 3D tensile strain on the detailed charge transfer behavior in metallic- gold-dispersed PNCG was investigated using in situ Hall-effect measurements.

## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Wide-angle XRD patterns of PNCG and 1 mol % Au/PNCG are shown in Figure 2a. The PNCG phase in all samples was attributed to the tetragonal structure of the Pr<sub>1.9</sub>NiO<sub>4</sub> (ICDD #01-080-0043), regardless of the presence of dispersed Au particles. No secondary phases or impurity phases were observed. Furthermore, because diffraction peaks from Au were observed, the dispersed Au seems to exist as a metallic phase, suggesting that the added Au did not react with the PNCG phase. Therefore, metallic particles of Au were successfully dispersed into the PNCG grains. Figure 2b magnifies the main diffraction peaks from 30 to 35° (in 2θ). The peak assigned to (1 1 3) diffraction shows a low-angle shoulder where it is marked by the arrowhead, and (2 0 0)/(1 0 4) peaks are broadened. Moreover, the diffraction peaks of Au/PNCG were shifted to a slightly lower angle compared with those of PNCG. This shift could be explained by the residual strain, which results from the difference in the thermal expansion coefficients, as shown in Figure 1, and the shift of the XRD peaks to lower angles suggests that tensile strain may be present in the sample. To confirm the split peaks shown in Figure 2b, the curve fitting of XRD pattern was performed. As discussed in the Supporting Information (Figures S1 and S2), it could be concluded that the broadening of (1 1 3) peaks could not be caused by the secondary phases and also the formation of another phase with orthorhombic phase of which diffraction peaks has not appeared in this angle. Distribution of cation was also measured with elemental mapping with EDX method (see Supporting Information Figure S3) and it was confirmed that the sample is uniform in composition and no segregation of cations was observed. Therefore, we assumed that the shoulder peaks are appeared by strain effects. As shown in SEM images in Figure 4, observed Au particles are 1−2 μm, which is not achieved a high dispersion state (as discussed later).

Introduction of the interstitial oxide ion means that the cations are oxidized due to the charge compensation (actually Ni ions are oxidized state as discussed with XPS results later). In order to confirm the influence of strain on the electronic properties of PNCG, the electrical conductivity was measured. Figure 5a shows Arrhenius plots of the total conductivity of PNCG and 1 mol % Au-dispersed PNCG. The electrical con- ductivity improved after dispersing the Au particles. This improvement was not caused by the improved density with Au addition, and the details are shown in the Supporting Information (Figure S6−S8). In order to confirm this change in conductivity, a Hall-effect measurement was carried out for each sample. The temperature dependences of the Hall coefficient in PNCG and Au/PNCG are shown in Figure 5b. The observed Hall coefficients were positive for both PNCG and Au/PNCG, indicating that holes are the primary charge carrier, regardless of the presence of Au particles. Figure 5c,d shows the temperature dependence of the electronic carrier concentration (denoted as n) and mobility (denoted as μ) in PNCG and 1 mol % Au/PNCG. The increased hole concentration could be explained by the increased interstitial oxygen amount discussed above, which can in turn be explained by the formation of oxidized cations of Pr<sup>4+</sup> and Ni<sup>3+</sup>. This suggests that tensile strain in PNCG could induce a change in the oxidation state of cations in the PNCG, as well as an increase in interstitial oxygen. Furthermore, a slightly decreased hole mobility was observed in Au-dispersed PNCG. It is well-known that the K<sub>2</sub>NiF<sub>4</sub> structure has two-dimensional conduction paths, which means that interstitial oxygen diffuses in the rock salt block and holes conduct in the perovskite block through the Ni and Pr cations.<sup>21,29</sup> Therefore, the reduced hole mobility might indicate that the enlarged distances along the a−b plane could make it difficult for the holes to migrate. Therefore, introduction of hole in perovskite block, which means Ni is in an oxidized state, more effectively contributes the increased hole conductivity. As mentioned in discussion of XPS results, there may be a large error for the estimation of the valence of Pr due to the significantly different measurement condition between the redox titration and the XPS measurement. Because the oxygen nonstoichiometry and valence state of cations are not static, it is requested to examine these states as a function of temperature and oxygen partial pressure to confirm the validity of the electrochemical measurements. The temperature dependence of oxygen nonstoichiometry of PNCG and 1 mol % Au/PNCG are shown in Figure 9a. The oxygen contents in 1 mol % Au/PNCG always show larger than those in PNCG in the temperature region from 573 to 1073 K. Also, the differences between with and without Au are almost similar at the all temperatures. This means the observed effects on electrochemical measurements by Au dispersion can be applied to the results at all temperatures. P<sub>O2</sub> dependence of oxygen nonstoichiometry also shows the similar tendency with the temperature dependence; that is, 1 mol % Au/PNCG shows larger oxygen content than PNCG as shown in Figure 9b. Therefore, it can be concluded that the observed effect by dispersing Au particles on the valence state of Ni could be applied to the all results of the electrochemical measurement. Furthermore, Figure 9c shows the double logarithmic plots between δ and oxygen partial pressure. The δ values of 1 mol % Au/PNCG shows lower P<sub>O2</sub> dependency compared with that of PNCG. This result is in good agreement with the suppressed P<sub>O2</sub> dependency of electrical conductivity for 1 mol % Au/PNCG as shown in Figure 5a. Therefore, oxygen nonstoichiometry measurement also supports the hole formation mechanism is under eq 2 and the result that the introduced holes are trapped at Ni-site. In any case, tensile strain introduced by Au dispersion affects the oxidation state of PNCG, which has a large influence on the hole concentration and mobility.

## 4. CONCLUSIONS

Hole migration properties in polycrystalline pellets with three-dimensional tensile strain were investigated using PNCG. It was found that tensile strain enhanced the electrical conductivity and that this enhancement was strongly related to changes in the interstitial oxygen nonstoichiometry and the valence number of the cations. The hole mobility decreased in the presence of tensile strain, which may be strongly related to the distance to the neighboring B-site cations. In this study, the effects of the strain were clearly observed independent of the crystallinity of the materials. This study reveals that the oxidation state of Ni in PNCG increased, and the amount of interstitial oxygen increased after applying a tensile strain. Therefore, hole conductivity was

increased after applying a tensile strain because of an increase in hole concentration even though hole mobility decreased slightly. Because electronic hole is charge compensation of oxide ion, the increased amount of hole suggests that interstitial oxygen could be increased resulting in the increased oxide ion conductivity. As a result, tensile strain in 3D is highly interesting for controlling the electric conductivity and oxygen diffusivity of the mixed ion conductor.
