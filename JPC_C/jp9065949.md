## LiBH4/SBA-15 Nanocomposites Prepared by Melt Infiltration under Hydrogen Pressure:

## Synthesis and Hydrogen Sorption Properties

Peter Ngene, Philipp Adelhelm, Andrew M. Beale, Krijn P. de Jong, and Petra E. de Jongh\*

Inorganic Chemistry and Catalysis, Debye Institute for Nanomaterials Science, Utrecht UniVersity,

Sorbonnelaan 16, 3584 CA Utrecht, The Netherlands

ReceiVed: July 13, 2009; ReVised Manuscript ReceiVed: January 29, 2010

Lithium borohydride (LiBH<sub>4</sub>) is a promising material for hydrogen storage, with a gravimetric hydrogen

content of 18.5%. However, the thermodynamics and kinetics of its hydrogen release and uptake need to be

improved before it can meet the requirements for mobile applications. In this study, we investigate the

confinement of LiBH<sub>4</sub> in ordered mesoporous SiO<sub>2</sub> and its effect on the hydrogen sorption properties. We

demonstrate that, only under hydrogen pressure, melt infiltration is an effective method for the synthesis of

LiBH<sub>4</sub>/SBA-15 nanocomposites. Our work clearly shows that formation of lithium silicates from LiBH<sub>4</sub> and

SiO<sub>2</sub> can effectively be suppressed by hydrogen. Thus, under hydrogen pressure, LiBH<sub>4</sub> can fully fill the

mesopores of SBA-15 while the long-range order of the mesopores is maintained. The confined LiBH<sub>4</sub> has

enhanced hydrogen desorption properties, with desorption starting at 150 °C. However, upon dehydrogenation,

SiO<sub>2</sub> and decomposition products of LiBH<sub>4</sub> react to form Li<sub>2</sub>SiO<sub>3</sub> and Li<sub>4</sub>SiO<sub>4</sub>, leading to irreversible hydrogen

loss.

## 1. Introduction

Hydrogen has been identified as a promising energy carrier. It is not associated with pollution when generated from renewable sources, such as solar and wind. For use as an energy carrier for automobile applications, its compact, safe, and efficient storage is an important issue.<sup>1</sup> The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has set a system target of 6 wt % of H<sub>2</sub> content by 2010 for mobile applications. Such a system should also be able to discharge hydrogen and be recharged at moderate conditions. However, none of the current storage methods and materials can satisfy all the DOE requirements for on-board application.

Storage in the form of a solid hydride is attractive from a safety and gravimetric density point of view. A promising class of materials is the complex metal hydrides, such as alanates and borohydrides. For instance LiBH<sub>4</sub> has a gravimetric hydrogen content of 18.5%. However, the compound is ther- modynamically very stable, releasing H<sub>2</sub> only at elevated temperatures (equilibrium typically 400 °C under 1 bar of H<sub>2</sub>). Moreover, it suffers from poor hydrogen uptake kinetics with limited rehydrogenation shown at 150 bar and 600 °C.<sup>2</sup>

Several methods to improve the H<sub>2</sub> sorption properties of LiBH<sub>4</sub> have been proposed. A first method deals with addition of metal, metal halides, oxides, amides, or metal hydrides to form composites or alloys after dehydrogenation.<sup>3–11</sup> This approach is sometimes referred to as “destabilization” of the LiBH<sub>4</sub> and can lower the overall enthalpy for dehydrogenation and improve reversibility. For example, Vajo et al.<sup>3,4</sup> obtained enhanced hydrogen sorption properties when MgH <sub>2</sub> was added to LiBH<sub>4.</sub> Formation of MgB<sub>2</sub> upon dehydrogenation reduces the dehydrogenation/rehydrogenation enthalpy by 25 kJ/(mol of H<sub>2</sub>) compared with pure LiBH<sub>4</sub>. The reversibility was also improved.

A second approach to improve the kinetics of hydrogen uptake and release is the use of catalysts. The addition of suitable catalysts has led to a significant improvement in the hydrogen sorption properties of materials, such as NaAlH<sub>4</sub><sup>11–13</sup> and MgH<sub>2</sub> .<sup>14,15</sup> Zu¨ttel et al.<sup>16</sup> showed that the addition of SiO<sub>2</sub> to LiBH<sub>4</sub> lowered the hydrogen desorption temperatures signifi- cantly, whereas Vajo et al. recently added TiCl<sub>3</sub> to improve the rate of H<sub>2</sub> absorption in the LiBH<sub>4</sub>/MgH<sub>2</sub> system.<sup>3</sup> Ming et al.<sup>6–8</sup> reported a significant decrease in the desorption temperature, increased desorption rates, and improved reversibility of LiBH<sub>4</sub> when doped with some metal halides and oxides. The system, LiBH<sub>4</sub> + 0.2MgCl<sub>2</sub> + 0.1TiCl<sub>3</sub>, desorbs 5 wt % of hydrogen at 60 °C and can be rehydrogenated to 4.5 wt %. However, the rehydrogenation is only possible at an elevated temperature of 600 °C (500 °C for LiBH<sub>4</sub> + 0.1TiF<sub>3</sub>) and 70 bar. Furthermore, the loss of boron in some of the systems reduces the reversible amount.

Reducing the size of materials to the nanoscale is another approach that is promising in terms of altering the kinetics and potentially also thermodynamics of hydrogen storage materials.<sup>17–24</sup> However, the practical application of this approach can be limited by grain growth and particle agglomeration during cycling. Recent literature reports show that confining the material in a nanoporous host might be beneficial. Gutowska et al. demonstrated that the hydrogen storage properties of ammonia borane (NH<sub>3</sub>BH<sub>3</sub>) could be improved by incorporating the compound into a mesoporous silica scaffold.<sup>20</sup> Reduced dehydrogenation temperatures and a change in the decomposi- tion pathway from that of bulk ammonia borane were observed. Balde´ et al.<sup>21,22</sup> showed that the hydrogen desorption of NaAlH<sub>4</sub> was greatly enhanced upon nanosizing using a carbon nanofiber support. Recent work by Vajo et al.<sup>23</sup> demonstrates that LiBH<sub>4</sub> incorporated into a carbon scaffold shows faster dehydrogenation than bulk LiBH <sub>4</sub>. Although these effects are generally not yet well-understood, they may be related to physical confinement, support effects, shorter diffusion distances, and particle size effects that come into play at the nanoscale. In this work, we investigate the possibility to confine LiBH<sub>4</sub> in ordered mesoporous silica and the effect of this confinement and intimate contact between LiBH<sub>4</sub> and porous SiO<sub>2</sub> on the hydrogen sorption properties of LiBH<sub>4</sub>. The feasibility of melt infiltration as a preparation route for nanocomposites has been shown previously for the Mg/C<sup>25</sup> and LiBH<sub>4</sub> /C under Ar pressure.<sup>23</sup> This approach has not been extended to LiBH<sub>4</sub>/SiO<sub>2</sub>, probably due to literature reports that LiBH<sub>4</sub> might react with silica upon heating to form lithium silicates.<sup>26,27</sup> In this paper, we prove that this synthesis procedure is also effective for LiBH<sub>4</sub>/SiO<sub>2</sub> but only if the melt infiltration is performed under hydrogen pressure high enough to prevent the decomposition of LiBH<sub>4</sub> during melting. The interest of using ordered meso- porous silica in this study is the possibility to vary its structural properties, such as pore size, specific pore volume, and surface area, and hence study the effects of these properties on the hydrogen desorption properties of the composite material. Furthermore, the pore order provides an extra tool to monitor the destruction of the pore structure by reaction.

## 2. Experimental Section

SBA-15 (amorphous SiO<sub>2</sub> with hexagonally ordered, uniform mesopores, for which the diameter can be varied from 5 to 9 nm) was prepared following the procedure of Zhao et al.,<sup>28</sup> whereas LiBH<sub>4</sub> (95% pure) was purchased from Acros Organics and used without further purification. Sample handling and storage was conducted under a N<sub>2</sub> atmosphere in a glovebox (contamination typically 1 ppm of O<sub>2</sub> and H<sub>2</sub> O). The required amounts of dried SBA-15 and LiBH<sub>4</sub> were mixed in a graphite sample holder and placed into a stainless steel autoclave. An initial pressure of 100 bar of H<sub>2</sub> was applied, and the sample was heated at 3 °C/min to 295 °C (LiBH<sub>4</sub> melting point ) 268 °C) and allowed to stay for 25 min at 295 °C at a final pressure of 180 bar of H<sub>2</sub>. The sample was then allowed to cool to room temperature, the hydrogen gas was released, and the samples were stored in the glovebox. Nanocomposites with nominal LiBH<sub>4</sub> loadings of 10, 25, 50, and 65 wt % were prepared and the samples were labeled according to the weight percentage of LiBH<sub>4</sub> in the mixture (100 × mass of LiBH<sub>4</sub>/total mass of the mixture). Physical mixtures were prepared by mixing LiBH<sub>4</sub> with dried SBA-15 in a mortar using a pestle.

Structural characterization was performed using N<sub>2</sub> phys- isorption and X-ray diffraction measurements in airtight sample holders. N<sub>2</sub> physisorption measurements were performed at -196 °C, using a Micromeritics Tristar 3000 apparatus. Prior to analysis, the samples were dried under vacuum for 14 h at 120 °C. The pore size distributions of the samples were calculated from the desorption branch using BJH theory with the Harkins and Jura thickness equation. XRD patterns were obtained at room temperature from 0.5 to 90° 2θ with a Bruker- AXS D-8 Advance X-ray diffractometer setup using Co KR<sub>1,2</sub> radiation with λ ) 1.79026 Å. We quantified the amount of crystalline phases, as well as estimated the amount of amorphous material, using a crystalline Si reference powder (nr 640 supplied by NIST, formerly known as NBS (U.S.A.), purity ) 99.9%, average particle size ) 10 μm). Quantitative phase analysis was performed by Rietveld profile fitting using the Powdercell program. Full details of the fitting are included in the Supporting Information.

## 3. Results

Synthesis of LiBH<sub>4</sub>/SBA-15 Nanocomposites. Figure 1 shows the XRD patterns of SBA-15 and melt infiltrated LiBH<sub>4</sub>/SBA-15 nanocomposites with different LiBH<sub>4</sub> weight loadings. SBA-15 shows a broad peak between 20° and 40° 2θ due to the presence of amorphous silica. Comparing the 50 wt % sample prepared under hydrogen (Figure 1, pattern d) and argon pressure (Figure 1, pattern f), it is clear that, in both cases, a large part of the LiBH<sub>4</sub> crystallinity is lost. However, for the sample prepared under argon pressure, the broad peak due to silica has completely disappeared while the silica broad peak is retained in the samples synthesized under hydrogen pressure. Furthermore, only diffraction lines due to LiBH<sub>4</sub> were observed, indicating that there is no formation of new crystalline phases. At weight loadings of LiBH<sub>4</sub> less than 25 wt %, no LiBH<sub>4</sub> diffraction lines were visible. This is in contrast with physical mixtures (not shown here) for which LiBH <sub>4</sub> diffraction lines were clearly seen even at a loading of 5 wt %. It suggests that, for the lower loadings, the LiBH<sub>4</sub> is confined in the pores, hence, not X-ray visible. For loadings of 25 wt % and above, crystalline LiBH<sub>4</sub> (probably located outside the pores) is detected.

The major peak at about 1.1° and two additional ones at higher angles due to the (100), (110), and (200) diffractions indicate well-ordered SBA-15 with a hexagonal unit cell parameter of 10.8 nm. The isotherm of the pristine material (Figure 3, curve a) shows a steep capillary condensation step and a hysteresis loop at a high relative pressure (above 0.6), typical of well-ordered mesoporous SBA-15. After the melt infiltration, the BET surface area and the pore volume of SBA-15 had decreased. However,

Therefore, the decrease in pore volume after melt infiltration indicates that the SBA-15 pores have been destroyed, blocked, or filled due to interaction with the molten LiBH<sub>4</sub>. XRD and SAXS proved that the SBA-15 was not damaged upon heating. Furthermore, Table 1 shows that the decrease in pore volume with increasing loading of LiBH<sub>4</sub> corresponds roughly to the expected volume of the added LiBH <sub>4</sub>. Hence, the pore volume loss can be ascribed to filling and possibly some blocking of the pores. Most likely, the capillary suction force enables the transport of the molten LiBH<sub>4</sub> into the pores of SBA-15, and on cooling, the molten LiBH<sub>4</sub> resolidifies. As no diffraction pattern was observed at low LiBH<sub>4</sub> loadings, the LiBH<sub>4</sub> in the pores shows no long-range crystallinity. The LiBH<sub>4</sub> diffraction lines observed at higher loading are most likely due to unconfined LiBH<sub>4.</sub> Above 50 wt % (corresponding to >90% pore filling), the pore volume is negligible, indicating that all the pores are filled. We can thus conclude that melt infiltration is an effective preparation technique for LiBH<sub>4</sub>/SBA-15 if and only if performed under hydrogen pressure.

Hydrogen Desorption Properties. Figure 4 compares the TPD profiles of pure LiBH<sub>4</sub> and melt infiltrated nanocomposites with 10, 50, and 65 wt % loadings of LiBH <sub>4</sub>. During the TPD runs, heat effects were observed, which can be ascribed to the transition from the orthorhombic to the hexagonal phase of LiBH<sub>4</sub> (around 110 °C) and the melting transition (around 270 °C), hence, at temperatures slightly lower than those reported for bulk LiBH <sub>4</sub>. The onset of dehydrogenation for bulk LiBH<sub>4</sub> is around 280 °C, close to its melting point and shows multiple desorption peaks, as also reported in the literature. The majority of the hydrogen is only released above 400 °C. For the melt infiltrated samples, H<sub>2</sub> desorption started at about 150 °C, which is more than a 100 °C reduction of the onset temperature compared with the bulk material.

The 10 and 50 wt % LiBH<sub>4</sub>/SBA-15 nanocomposites display a single broad desorption peak with a maximum between 280 and 300 °C and a shoulder at 300-350 °C. The 65 wt % sample has, in addition, a hydrogen release peak around 380 °C. Above 50 wt %, all the SBA-15 pores are filled and some bulk LiBH<sub>4</sub> is expected to be outside the pores. Indeed, for the extra peak in the hydrogen desorption from the 65 wt % nanocomposites, the release temperature is similar to that of the first major release peak for bulk LiBH<sub>4</sub>. However, remarkably, no second bulklike release peak is observed in the temperature range of 400-450 °C. This indicates that, although, for the 65 wt % nanocomposite sample, part of the LiBH<sub>4</sub> is outside the pores, the release of hydrogen is still influenced by the presence of the SiO<sub>2</sub>, modifying the multiple step release normally observed for pure LiBH<sub>4</sub>.

Clearly, confining LiBH<sub>4</sub> in the pores of SBA-15 leads to lower hydrogen desorption temperatures. This can have several reasons. First, it might be that reducing the size of the LiBH<sub>4</sub> to nanoscale alters its dehydrogenation properties, as demon- strated for LiBH<sub>4</sub>/C nanocomposites.<sup>23</sup> Second, it might be due to catalytic effects of SiO <sub>2</sub> (as originally claimed by Zu¨ttel et al.).<sup>16</sup> A third reason for the change in desorption could be due to reaction with SiO<sub>2</sub> during heating, as reported in the literature.<sup>26,27</sup>

Furthermore, the capacity decreases with increasing cycling, possibly due to boron loss, as reported by Ming et al. <sup>6,8</sup>

To study the effects of the structural properties of SBA-15 on the desorption properties of the nanocomposites in more detail, experiments using SBA-15 with different pore sizes and volumes were performed for nanocomposites with a LiBH<sub>4</sub> loading of 50 wt %. Figure 6 shows that the structural properties of the porous silica have two remarkable effects on the hydrogen release properties of the nanocomposites. First, the hydrogen desorption profile is changed, with lower hydrogen release temperatures favored by high surface areas/pore volumes for the same given LiBH<sub>4</sub> wt % loading. Second, the onset of hydrogen desorption shows a clear contribution at temperatures far below the bulk LiBH<sub>4</sub> melting temperature, especially for the SiO <sub>2</sub> with the highest surface area and pore volume. These effects will be discussed in more detail in the following section.

However, for hydrogen storage materials, especially those prepared by ball-milling, several nonclassical (de)hydrogenation catalysts seem very effective,<sup>29</sup> although the mechanism or exact action of the catalyst is often not clear yet. Since the first papers, several authors have discussed that LiBH<sub>4</sub> might actually react with the SiO<sub>2</sub> upon heating, forming silicates. Evidence for the formation of silicates was demon- strated by means of XRD and in situ synchrotron radiation powder X-ray diffraction.<sup>26,27</sup> Also, experiments in which TiO<sub>2</sub> was added to the LiBH<sub>4</sub> show that lithium titanate was formed after heating the sample.<sup>30</sup> Hence, at the moment, it is generally assumed that reaction between LiBH<sub>4</sub> and SiO<sub>2</sub> is kinetically hindered at room temperature but, upon heating, takes place, especially when the LiBH<sub>4</sub> starts melting, leading to an irreversible reaction to lithium silicates. <sup>26</sup>

In this paper, we use the XRD signature of the pore order in mesoporous SBA-15 as a clear fingerprint whether reaction involving SiO<sub>2</sub> has taken place or not. This means that it is possible to detect the reaction, even if no crystalline silicates are formed. The results presented in this paper show that indeed melt infiltration of SBA-15 (a high surface area and amorphous, hence, reactive form of SiO<sub>2</sub>) under Ar is not possible. The SBA-15 structure was destroyed after heating to 295 °C in the

presence of LiBH<sub>4</sub>. This would be in line with the assumption that, as soon as the kinetic barriers have been overcome (because the LiBH<sub>4</sub> starts melting), fast reaction is possible. However, if we use hydrogen pressures well above the calculated equilibrium pressure at 300 °C (using data from ref 2), the reaction with the SiO<sub>2</sub> was effectively suppressed. Hence, the presence of molten LiBH<sub>4</sub> alone is not enough for reaction with the SiO<sub>2</sub>. Apparently, molten LiBH<sub>4</sub> does not react directly with SiO<sub>2</sub>, but, most likely, its decomposition products. In the literature, intermediate phases are suggested that can be formed during thermal dehydrogenation of LiBH<sub>4</sub>.<sup>16,31–36</sup> Most likely, one of these intermediate products might be the reactive species. Under hydrogen pressure, the decomposition is hindered and thus melt infiltration can be performed even above 300 °C without any silicate formation.

Unfortunately, upon decomposition of the LiBH<sub>4</sub> in the nanocomposites, reaction takes place with the SiO<sub>2</sub>. The multiple hydrogen desorption peaks observed at higher LiBH<sub>4</sub> loadings (Figure 4) can be ascribed to reactions, first, to form Li<sub>2</sub> SiO3 around 300 °C and subsequent reaction with the Li<sub>2</sub>SiO<sub>3</sub> to form Li<sub>4</sub>SiO4.<sup>8,26,27</sup> As this leads to very stable lithium silicates, this reaction is irreversible. The partial reversibility observed in the composite with a LiBH <sub>4</sub> loading of 65 wt % is probably due to partial rehydrogenation of LiBH<sub>4</sub> in excess of the stoichiometric amount required for complete reaction with SiO<sub>2</sub>. The further decrease in capacity on cycling might be due to boron loss, as reported in the literature.<sup>6,8–37</sup> For the 65 wt %, it is expected that the LiBH<sub>4</sub> in excess of the stoichiometric amount required to completely react with SiO<sub>2</sub> will exhibit bulk dehydrogenation behavior. However, the desorption profile (Figure 4) shows a clear departure from bulk behavior, with only one clearly defined hydrogen release peak instead of the multiple peak desorption profile observed for bulk LiBH <sub>4</sub>. To test whether this was due to interaction with the lithium silicates in the sample, we made a physical mixture of LiBH<sub>4</sub> and Li<sub>4</sub>SiO<sub>4</sub>. In this case, a similar desorption profile was obtained, showing that departure from bulk behavior is due to the interaction of the LiBH <sub>4</sub> with Li<sub>4</sub>SiO4.

Figure 6 shows the effects for a given molar ratio (LiBH<sub>4</sub>/SiO<sub>2</sub> ) 3:8), which roughly corresponds to the stoichiometry needed to fully form Li<sub>4</sub>SiO<sub>4</sub>. However, SiO<sub>2</sub> with different pore sizes is used, which enabled us to distinguish between the impact of nanoconfinement in the pores (which will be more pro- nounced for the smaller pore sizes) and the kinetics of the reaction to form lithium silicates (which will be more pro- nounced for the higher pore volumes/surface areas, hence, the samples with the largest pores). As seen in Figure 6, both the onset of dehydrogenation and the TPD profile for the 50 wt % sample change as the structural properties of the SBA-15 are changed. Interestingly, for the 50 wt % nanocomposite with the highest specific surface area and pore volume of SBA-15, the two steps in reaction 1 occur almost simultaneously. This is probably due to the fact that all the LiBH<sub>4</sub> is confined in the pores of SBA-15 and more intimate contact and shorter diffusion distance increase the rate of the reaction. For the nanocomposites with lower pore volumes, a large part of the LiBH<sub>4</sub> is outside the porous SiO<sub>2</sub>, hence, leading to slower reaction due to diffusion limitations. The reaction to form lithium silicates might be prevented by using a less reactive matrix, such as carbon. Another possibility is to passivate the SBA-15, for instance, by silanation, as shown effectively in the case of impregnation with sodium alanates. <sup>38</sup>

## 5. Conclusions

Melt infiltration as a preparation method for LiBH<sub>4</sub>/SBA-15 nanocomposites has been investigated and the hydrogen sorption properties of the nanocomposites determined. It was shown that melt infiltration is effective only if performed under hydrogen pressure to avoid the decomposition of LiBH <sub>4</sub>. Surprisingly, molten LiBH<sub>4</sub> can fill the pores of SBA-15, forming LiBH<sub>4</sub>/SBA-15 nanocomposites while the ordered porous structure of SBA-15 remains intact. Only upon decomposition of the nanostructured LiBH<sub>4</sub> does reaction with the SiO<sub>2</sub> takes place, leading to irreversible hydrogen loss. Monitoring the pore order in SBA-15 is a simple and sensitive probe to follow the reaction between the LiBH<sub>4</sub> and SiO<sub>2</sub>, even if no crystalline products are formed. Varying the structural properties of the ordered mesoporous SiO<sub>2</sub> allowed further understanding of the reaction and the different products formed.
