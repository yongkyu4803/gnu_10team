In this work, three types of MnO<sub>2</sub> nanostructures, viz., microsphere/nanosheet core-corona hierarchical

architectures, one-dimensional (1D) nanorods, and nanotubes, have been synthesized employing a simple

hydrothermal process. The formation mechanisms have been rationalized. The materials have been thoroughly

characterized by X-ray diffraction, Brunauer-Emmett-Teller spectrometry, field-emission scanning electron

miscroscopy, energy dispersive spectroscopy, and transmission electron microscopy. The microsphere/nanosheet

core-corona hierarchical structures are found to be the layered birnessite-type MnO<sub>2</sub>, while 1D nanorods

and nanotubes are of the R-MnO<sub>2</sub> phase. These MnO<sub>2</sub> nanostructures are used as a model system for studying

the shape/phase-dependent electrocatalytic properties for the oxygen reduction reaction, which have be

investigated by cyclic and linear sweep voltammetry. It is found that R-MnO<sub>2</sub> nanorods/tubes possess largely

enhanced electrocatalytic activity compared to birnessite-type MnO<sub>2</sub> core-corona spheres despite the latter

having a much higher specific surface area. The vast difference in electrocatalytic activity is discussed in

terms of crystal structure, oxygen adsorption mode, and exposed crystal facets.

## Introduction

The oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) is of prime importance in view of its practical applications in green energy conversion/ storage systems ranging from fuel cells to metal/air batteries.<sup>1-5</sup> At present, materials containing precious metals such as platinum, palladium, and gold are the most successful electro- catalysts for the ORR.<sup>3,6-9</sup> The high costs and limited availability of the requisite raw materials have retarded large-scale com- mercial applications. This has led to an urgent need for electrocatalysts made of inexpensive and more abundant materi- als but with comparable activity to precious metal based electrocatalysts. Indeed, numerous nonprecious materials, such as carbon, polymer, transitional metals, and oxides, have been suggested as alternative electrocatalysts for the ORR.<sup>1,2,4,10,11</sup> Among these materials, manganese oxides have drawn particular attention because of their low cost, high activity in alkaline media, and nontoxicity.<sup>12-20</sup> For example, Bruce and co-workers recently reported a successful prototype of a rechargeable Li/ O<sub>2</sub> battery using an R-MnO<sub>2</sub> nanowires catalyst, which can deliver a much higher capacity than conventional rechargeable lithium batteries.<sup>21</sup> Additionally, Zhuang and co-workers have developed a high-performance OH<sup>-</sup> conductive polymer and used it to build an efficient prototype of a nonprecious metal alkaline polymer electrolyte fuel cell (APEFC). <sup>22</sup>

It is generally observed that nanostructured materials exhibit favorable enhanced kinetics and activity derived from their large surface area and size effects.<sup>23-25</sup> Thus, in addition to chemical composition, a variety of factors, including the crystal phases present, structure, morphology, oxidation state, and surface area,

will remarkably impact physicochemical properties of these catalysts.<sup>26-29</sup> Nanostructured manganese oxides are promising low-cost materials for electrocatalysts for the ORR, but their widespread implementation hinges upon development of catalyst systems with high activity. In addition, structure-activity relationships for nanostructured MnO<sub>2</sub> for the ORR are required to elucidate the factors that determine catalyst performance.

The main challenge for such studies comes from the limited number of synthetic routes for controlled synthesis of MnO<sub>2</sub> nanostructures. These challenges have stimulated broad-based research efforts by many groups worldwide devoted to the synthesis of nanostructured manganese oxides.<sup>30-35</sup> Herein, we report a facile one-pot hydrothermal approach that permits exquisite control over the crystal structure and morphology of MnO<sub>2</sub> nanostructures. Specifically, simply varying the reaction temperature in our hydrothermal process, enables synthesis of three different MnO<sub>2</sub> nanostructures (microsphere/nanosheet core-corona hierarchical architecture, one-dimensional nanorod, and nanotube) with controlled crystal phases and morphologies. The formation mechanisms for each of these products are rationalized. The as-prepared MnO<sub>2</sub> nanostructures are found to possess excellent electrocatalytic activity in alkaline media for the apparent four-electron reduction of oxygen. The vast difference in electroactivity from different products clearly attests to the importance of engineering the morphology, crystal phase and tunnel structure of MnO <sub>2</sub> nanostructures.

Electrochemical Measurement. Electrochemical measure- ments for ORR were carried out on an Autolab electrochemical workstation (model PGSTAT30) at room temperature in a three- electrode system using 0.1 M KOH as an electrolyte. The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was recorded at a scan rate of 5 mV s<sup>-1</sup> with a rotating glassy carbon disk electrode (RDE, 4 mm in diameter) as the working electrode, and the rotation rate of the disk was varied between 100 and 2500 rpm. To prepare the working electrode, 2 mg of as-prepared catalyst was dispersed ultrasonically in 1 mL of diluted Nafion alcohol solution (0.05 wt %), and 20 μL of the suspension was pipetted onto a glassy carbon substrate. Pt wire was used as the counter electrode. The reference electrode was a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) in the same electrolyte as the electrochemical cell. All potentials in this work are referred to RHE. Prior to measurement, O<sub>2</sub> was bubbled directly into the cell for at least 15 min to saturate the solution. During each measurement, O<sub>2</sub> was flushed over the cell solution.

## Results and Discussion

To study the growth mechanism of nano-MnO<sub>2</sub>, experiments are carried out at different temperatures for the same duration (12 h). From the XRD pattern in Figure 1a, the product prepared at 100 °C is identified to be pure layered birnessite-type MnO<sub>2</sub> (JCPDS No. 80-1098, monoclinic, C2/m, a ) 5.15 Å, b ) 2.84 Å, c ) 7.17 Å). When the temperature is increased to 120 °C or higher, the products are found to be purely R-MnO<sub>2</sub> (JCPDS No. 44-0141, tetragonal, I4/m, a ) b ) 9.78 Å, c ) 2.86 Å). In general, MnO<sub>2</sub> can form many polymorphs by interlinking the basic MnO<sub>6</sub> octahedra in different configurations. Figure 1b shows the schematic crystal structures of the two types of MnO<sub>2</sub> .<sup>36</sup> Birnessite-type MnO<sub>2</sub>, also denoted as δ-MnO<sub>2</sub> in the literature, has a two-dimensional (2D) lamellar structure with an interlayer distance of 0.73 nm. Structurally, R-MnO<sub>2</sub> is constructed from the double chains of edge-sharing MnO<sub>6</sub> octahedra which are linked at the corners to form (2 × 2) + (1 × 1) tunnel structures (the sizes of the 2 × 2 and 1 × 1 tunnels are 0.46 and 0.189 nm, respectively). These tunnels extend in a direction parallel to the c axis of the tetragonal unit cell. When the reaction temperature is increased to 120 °C, the product is tetragonal R-MnO<sub>2</sub> (Figure 1a). As a result, the morphology changes to tetragonal nanorods, as depicted in the SEM images (parts c and d of Figure 2). The as-synthesized R-MnO <sub>2</sub> nanorods, typically 2-3 μm in length, have a square cross-section with an edge length in the range of 50-100 nm. The BET surface area of these nanorods is measured to be about 19 m<sup>2</sup> g<sup>-1</sup>. Figure 3c displays a TEM image of a typical nanorod indicating a smooth surface, and a SAED pattern (inset) based on a single nanorod indicating the single-crystalline nature. An average d spacing of about 0.5 nm is observed from the lattice- resolved TEM image (Figure 3d). This d-spacing is consistent with the spacing between (200) planes. The SAED pattern and HRTEM analysis suggest that the nanorods grow along the [001] direction (c axis). In general, a higher temperature is favorable for anisotropic growth of crystal which gives higher aspect ratios.<sup>33</sup> The rolling-cum-phase transformation process can be employed to depict the formation mechanism of the R-MnO<sub>2</sub> nanorods.<sup>33</sup> Specifically, during the initial stage of reaction, the relatively high K<sup>+</sup> concentration facilitates the formation of 2D birnessite-MnO<sub>2</sub> nanosheets. The lamellar nanosheets tend to curl at an elevated temperature because the layered birnessite-MnO<sub>2</sub> is a metastable phase. Eventually, the layered structure collapses into 2 × 2 tunnel structure of R-MnO<sub>2</sub>, and the dissolution-crystallization process increases the size and aspect ratio of the final product.

Although R-MnO<sub>2</sub> is also formed at 140 °C (Figure 1a), the morphology transforms to 1D nanotubes as shown in parts e and f of Figure 2 and Figure 3e. The BET surface area of these nanotubes is about 25 m<sup>2</sup> g<sup>-1</sup>. Figure 2f reveals that each nanotube has a square open end. HRTEM (Figure 3f) and SAED analysis (Figure 3e inset) suggest that the nanotubes possess the same growth direction as the nanorods prepared at 120 °C. On the basis of the above observations, the formation process of the nanotubes can be deduced as “dissolution or etching” of each nanorod from both ends inward along the long axis ([001] direction in this case), which leads to the formation of hollow tubes.<sup>34</sup> A similar process has been also observed in the formation of ZnO nanorings and R-Fe<sub>2</sub>O3 nanotubes.<sup>37,38</sup> In general, a higher temperature favors the occurrence of the dissolution or etching process. <sup>37,38</sup> Therefore, nanorods are formed at 120 °C, while all the products prepared at 140, 160, 180, and 200 °C are R-MnO<sub>2</sub> nanotubes (see Figures S2 and S3 of Supporting Information).

Time-dependent experiments are also carried out at 140 °C in order to further understand the growth process (see Figures S4 and S5 of Supporting Information). After 1 h of hydrothermal reaction, the product consists predominantly of birnessite-MnO<sub>2</sub> with a microsphere/nanosheet hierarchical architecture. With increasing duration, the anisotropic growth facilitates the formation of R-MnO<sub>2</sub> nanorods. Then the dissolution process occurs from the tip toward the center along the [001] direction. Longer duration is required to form completely hollow R-MnO<sub>2</sub> nanotubes. The above observations are consistent with the recent findings.<sup>34,35</sup>

Previous studies have confirmed that the overall 4-electron pathway of the ORR (reaction 5-7) in MnO<sub>2</sub> initiates from the electroreduction of MnO<sub>2</sub> to MnOOH (reaction 5).<sup>17-19</sup> This reduction is unfavorable in MnO <sub>2</sub> phases which have a high Mn-O bond strength since it makes the initial reduction more difficult. The average Mn-O bond lengths of birnessite-MnO<sub>2</sub> and R-MnO<sub>2</sub> are about 1.94 and 1.98 Å, respectively.<sup>29</sup> Therefore, birnessite-MnO<sub>2</sub> shows a higher Mn-O bond strength, which eventually results in a lower electrocatalytic activity. It is also speculated that crystal phase (channel structure) could be another determining factor for electrocatalytic activity. Generally, the electrocatalytic activity is in part determined by the reversible adsorption/desorption of reactants/products into and out of the open channel structure. The large interlayer spacing (0.7 nm) in birnessite-MnO<sub>2</sub> enables adsorp- tion of more O<sub>2</sub> molecules, but the MnO<sub>6</sub> octahedra sheet of birnessite-MnO<sub>2</sub> is much denser than that of R-MnO<sub>2</sub>. Overall the reducibility of birnessite-MnO<sub>2</sub> is lower than that of R-MnO <sub>2</sub>.<sup>29</sup> Therefore, the ORR reaction in birnessite-MnO<sub>2</sub> could be hindered by the excessively strong adsorption of reactant. The pronounced difference in electrocatalytic activity between R-MnO<sub>2</sub> and birnessite-MnO<sub>2</sub> is further speculated from the different adsorption orientations of molecular oxygen in the two phases.<sup>42,43</sup> The O-O bond length in molecular oxygen is about 0.12 nm which is comparable to the size of (1 × 1) tunnels in the R-MnO<sub>2</sub>.<sup>44</sup> Hence the adsorbed oxygen could be accom- modated in the (1 × 1) channels via the bridge mode. Since the bridge-mode adsorption (also known as the Yeager Model) is usually believed to favor the breaking of O-O bond of molecular oxygen and thus a direct 4-electron reduction of oxygen,<sup>43-45</sup> the R-MnO<sub>2</sub> with (1 × 1) tunnels can therefore provide enhanced kinetics for oxygen reduction.

The anomalous difference in electroactivity may also partly originate from the intrinsic difference of activity on different crystal facets. For example, birnessite-MnO <sub>2</sub> nanosheets and tetragonal R-MnO <sub>2</sub> nanorods/tubes have a large amount of exposed (001) and (200) surface, respectively. Clearly further

investigation in this direction is required, ideally combined with computational calculations. Although MnO<sub>2</sub> nanostructures reported here are still inferior to precious metals as electrocata- lyst for the ORR, our findings suggest that engineering the shape, crystal phase, and microstructure could be an effective approach for enhancing the activity of nonprecious electrocatalyst for the ORR. Our preliminary results show that further improvement of electrocatalytic activity can be achieved by making MnO<sub>2</sub>-based nanocomposites.

## Conclusions

In summary, we report a facile one-pot hydrothermal route for synthesis of three MnO<sub>2</sub> nanostructures and their self-assembled vertical arrays on substrates. By simply adjusting the hydrothermal reaction temperature, we can tailor the morphology and crystal phase from birnessite-type MnO<sub>2</sub> with a microsphere/nanosheet core-corona hierarchical architecture to R-MnO<sub>2</sub> with 1D nanorod or nanotube structures. Importantly, for the first time, we report a new finding that there is a strong dependence of the electroactivity of MnO<sub>2</sub> for the ORR on its crystal phase. The R-MnO<sub>2</sub> exhibits superior ORR electroactivity compared to birnessite-type MnO<sub>2</sub>.This finding adds to our understanding of MnO<sub>2</sub>-based catalysts and further development of low-cost and high-efficiency alkaline polymer electrolyte fuel cells and metal-air batteries.
