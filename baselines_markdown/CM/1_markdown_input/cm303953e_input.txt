1-002	Organic semiconductors have great potential to be used for flexible, low cost, large area applications.[1]
1-003	Specifically, polymer semiconductors are good candidates because they can be easily processed by spin coating and printing methods to generate uniform thin films for large area applications.[2]
1-004	Polymeric semiconducting materials have several common features, such as a conjugated backbone to facilitate the passage
1-005	of charge (either holes or electrons) and solubilizing group(s)
1-006	to make the polymer processable.
1-007	Fused thiophene based polymers, such as P2TDC17FT4, have previously been reported[3,4] with field effect mobilities, up to 0.4 cm2/V·s.
1-008	Electrophoretic displays (EPD) have been demonstrated successfully with P2TDC17FT4 as the semiconductor in the active transistor layer.[5]
1-009	In the past few years, remarkable improvements in charge carrier mobility have been achieved by using push−pull structures in polymer semiconductor design.[6]
1-010	Recently, diketopyrrolopyrrole (DPP) based polymers have been reported with mobilities greater than 2 cm2/V·s,[7,8] with some reported close to 10 cm2/V·s. 9 These breakthroughs in reported mobility have brought the performance of organic semiconductors above that of amorphous silicon transistors,

1-013	For any new semiconducting material to be considered for potential commercial use it must meet the following three main requirements.
1-014	First it must be readily available.
1-015	This requires a facile and scalable synthesis and purification procedure.
1-016	This in turn relies on the availability of starting materials and their easy synthesis and purification on a large scale.
1-017	Second, the material must be easily processed into device architectures.
1-018	For polymeric semiconductors this means good solubility in industrially acceptable solvents.
1-019	Environmental and regulatory concerns often restrict the use of chlorinated solvents, thus requiring solubility and processability in nonchlorinated solvent systems.
1-020	Ease of processing also includes readily forming good films after deposition from the chosen solvent, easy solvent removal, and formation of desirable film morphology.
1-021	The third prerequisite is to have appropriate performance once in a device.
1-022	This includes mobility and threshold voltage, but also stability over time, both operating and on the shelf.

2-002	Design.
2-003	Herein, the synthesis and characterization of a fused thiophene-diketopyrrolopyrrole based semiconducting poly- mer, PTDPPTFT4, is presented.
2-004	A fused thiophene-DPP based polymer was chosen to combine the benefits of the very stable fused thiophene component[11] with the advantages of a push−pull system created by combining with a DPP moiety.
2-005	The alkyl-substituted DPP portion of this polymer makes use of two C16H33 straight-chain alkyl groups rather than branched side-chains.
2-006	This was an intentional choice based on several factors.
2-007	A number of semiconducting materials made from branched side-chain substituted DPPs have been re- ported.[7,8,12−17] They have two significant drawbacks.
2-008	First, branched side-chain monomer synthesis is not trivial and is difficult to scale up.
2-009	Even though branched side-chains impart better solubility to the final polymer, the greater solubility of the DPP monomer makes purification from residual branched alkyl halide extremely difficult as chromatographic separation is needed, which is undesirable for scaling up the synthesis.
2-010	The other main drawback of branched side-chain substituted DPP polymers is their low thermal stability, compared to their linear chain equivalents.[18]
2-011	This fact has been exploited by other researchers to produce thermally cleavable side-chains.[14,19]
2-012	Moreover, branched side-chains have been found to increase the π−π stacking distance and resulted in lower charge carrier mobility.[20]
2-013	The C16H33 chain was selected because it is close in length to the C 17H35 chains on the fused thiophene portion of the material and it is long enough to impart significant solubility.
2-014	It is synthesized from the bromide, which is between 1 and 2 orders of magnitude cheaper than C15 or C17 bromides.
2-015	The two C17H35 chains of the FT4 were made from C18 starting materials, with one carbon from each chain being incorporated into the outer fused rings.[21]
2-016	The version with all four side-chains the same length (all C17) was also synthesized on a small scale, for comparison.
2-017	It exhibited very similar molecular weights and solubilities to those of PTDPPTFT4.
2-018	Synthesis.
2-019	The synthesis of PTDPPTFT4 was achieved by a Stille coupling of a fused thiophene monomer and a diketopyrrolopyrrole (DPP) co-monomer.
2-020	Detailed synthetic procedures for each step can be found in the Supporting Information.
2-021	The fused thiophene component may be synthesized[21] with either halogen[3] (most commonly bromide) or alkyl tin[22] substituents.
2-022	The difficulty of synthesizing the tin substituted version of the DPP makes this an unattractive option; thus, the bromothienyl-DPP was used as a co- monomer, combined with the tin-FT4, for the synthesis of PTDPPTFT4.
2-023	The ditin-FT4 was synthesized[22] from the dibromo-FT43 in hexane, using butyl lithium to perform a lithium halogen exchange, followed by the addition of trimethyltin chloride (Scheme 1).
2-024	On small scale test reactions of 5 g or less, the additions may be carried out at −78 °C. However, performing this reaction at such a low temperature with a larger scale gave poor results because the fused thiophene has a tendency to precipitate out, preventing further reaction.
2-025	This issue may be addressed by carrying out the reaction at 0 °C as long as the temperature is carefully controlled during addition of the butyl lithium.
2-026	This reaction requires a double lithium halogen exchange to occur.
2-027	This is actually very difficult on an FT4 species.
2-028	After the initial addition was completed it was
2-029	necessary to reflux the system for several hours to drive the double lithium halogen exchange to completion.
2-030	After cooling to 0 °C again the reaction was completed by dropwise addition of the trimethyltin chloride, carefully controlling the addition rate to prevent excessive heat evolution.
2-031	After precipitation and recrystallization, the Bis-trimethyltin-FT4 product was obtained in a 97% yield.
2-032	The bromothienyl-DPP synthesis was based on literature procedures.[23,24]
2-033	Scheme 2 shows the synthesis of the specific
2-034	bromothienyl-DPP used to make the semiconducting material studied herein.
2-035	Thiophene-carbonitrile and diisopropyl succi- nate were combined to form a thiophene substituted DPP.
2-036	This was done in tert-amyl alcohol, with a yield of 82%.
2-037	The basic thienyl DPP was then N-alkylated using a straight chain alkyl bromide (88% yield), followed by α-bromination of the thiophene groups (90% yield), to make it suitable as a Stille coupling co-monomer.
2-038	The final material (bromothienyl- DC16DPP) was obtained in a 65% overall yield for the three steps.
2-039	The target polymer PTDPPTFT4 was synthesized by a Stille coupling, using a palladium catalyst with o-tolyl phosphine ligands (Scheme 3).
2-040	Many different catalyst-ligand combina- tions were tested during the development of this synthesis, however, only those reactions using o-tolyl phosphine ligands produced polymer with molecular weights (Mn) in excess of 15 KDa.
2-041	The synthesis may be carried out in a number of solvents, both chlorinated and nonchlorinated.
2-042	Microwave synthesis in toluene for 2 h at 160 °C has proved effective.
2-043	Microwave synthesis allows higher than otherwise achievable temperatures because of the use of sealed tubes allowing overpressure and facilitating rapid reaction.
2-044	However, larger scales (>100g) are not currently practical in commercially available microwave reaction systems.
2-045	In such cases thermal
2-046	based batch reactions may be used to synthesize the polymer.
2-047	Removal of oxygen is required to make high quality polymer.
2-048	Reactions run in air generally yielded lower molecular weights and broader polydispersities.
2-049	Purification of the polymer was achieved by precipitation into a stirred mixture of methanol and acetyl acetone, followed by Soxhlet extractions with acetone and then hexanes, before the polymer itself was extracted from the Soxhlet with chloroform, leaving behind any insoluble material.
2-050	Other solvents (e.g., nonchlorinated solvents) may be substituted for the extraction if so desired.
2-051	Toluene was tested for use in the extraction process and found to be suitable.
2-052	Gel Permeation Chromatography (GPC).
2-053	GPC analysis of the different polymer batches of PTDPPTFT4 made under different conditions, using different solvents, catalysts, temper- atures, and durations, provided interesting observations.
2-054	Previous FT4 based polymers, such as P2TDC17FT4,[4] show aggregation even at relatively high temperatures, as does this new polymer.
2-055	Increasing temperature can help break up aggregates and allow measurements to be made on the nonaggregated polymer chains.
2-056	Solutions of P2TDC17FT4 in di- or trichlorobenzene are a deep purple-red at room temperature, but are a pale orange at elevated temperatures when the aggregates have been fully broken up.
2-057	The GPC for P2TDC17FT4 was carried out in 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene at 160 °C, to avoid the influence of aggregates on the apparent molecular weight.
2-058	However, PTDPPTFT4 does not show obvious changes in color, remaining dark green from room temperature up to the boiling point of 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene (214 °C).
2-059	Using o-tolyl phosphine ligands in the synthesis of PTDPPTFT4 gave higher molecular weights.
2-060	However, GPC analyses run at 160 °C showed unusually high polydispersities, and higher molecular weight samples showed bimodal distributions of molecular weights.
2-061	This is unexpected from a highly efficient polymerization reaction between molar equivalents of two divalent monomers.
2-062	This suggests, even though 160 °C was sufficient to disrupt aggregates in P2TDC17FT4, this was not the case in higher weight samples of PTDPPTFT4.
2-063	Another significant indicator of the presence of aggregation in higher molecular weight samples was the marked shift in change of refractive index (n) with concentration (c) (dn/dc) observed when comparing lower molecular weight samples of PTDPPTFT4 exhibiting a monomodal molecular weight distribution with higher molecular weight samples exhibiting a bimodal molecular weight distribution.
2-064	The effect of temperature on aggregation of
2-065	DPP based polymers has previously been observed.[9,17,25]
2-066	A study of the effect of temperature on the apparent molecular weight of PTDPPTFT4 showed that the bimodal distribution was a result of aggregation.
2-067	Figure 1 shows GPC traces from a
2-068	variable temperature GPC study on a single 1 mg/mL solution
2-069	molecular weight data relative to polystyrene standards from 140 to 200 °C. The polydispersity dropped from a peak value of almost nine, down to slightly less than two.
2-070	A polydispersity of less than the theoretical value of two is reasonable, because the lower weight fractions were removed by solvent extraction, post-synthesis, but before GPC measurements.
2-071	The molecular weight determined by using an elevated GPC system temperature (200 °C) is providing a more accurate value (within the restrictions imposed by the use of polystyrene standards), by eliminating the influence of aggregation on the results.
2-072	Fused thiophene and DPP based copolymers tend to aggregate, with different specific species aggregating to various degrees.
2-073	It is therefore important to measure molecular weight at elevated temperatures for those polymers exhibiting high PDI at lower temperatures.

2-096	Thermal Stability.
2-097	The polymer (PTDPPTFT4) exhibits excellent thermal stability.
2-098	Thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) shows that degradation does not begin until 310 °C in air and
2-099	not until 407 °C in nitrogen.
2-100	This is comparable to the thermal stability of previously published fused thiophene polymer semiconductors,[4] with the added advantage of the increased solubility of this new polymer.
2-101	Polymer thermal behavior was also evaluated by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC).
2-102	PTDPPTFT4 shows a melting transition around 40−45 °C, attributed to side-chain melting.
2-103	The presence of a side-chain melting transition is an indication that the side-chains possess sufficient overlap to form crystalline regions.
2-104	PTDPPTFT4 has
2-105	a slightly higher side-chain melting temperature than P2TDC17FT4, which exhibits side-chain melting at 0−20 °C (see Supporting Information for DSC traces).
2-106	At higher temperatures the DSC of PTDPPTFT4 is featureless, similar
2-107	to other FT4 based polymers, unlike other previously reported polymers, such as pBTCT-C10, pBTTT-C10, or pADBT-C14, which each show at least one significant transition between 100
2-108	and 300 °C.[26]

2-127	Film Characterization.
2-128	Figure 5 shows the grazing-incident X-ray diffraction (GIXD) patterns of a PTDPPTFT4 film spin- coated on a crystalline octadecyltrimethoxylsilane (OTS)-treated silicon substrate.[30,31]
2-129	The polymer film exhibited well- defined (100), (200), and (300) diffraction peaks along the out-
2-130	of-plane Qz axis.
2-131	This reveals that the polymer film possesses a highly ordered lamellar structure.
2-132	Besides the out of plane peaks, the film also displayed two in-plane diffraction peaks at 1.48 and 1.69 Å−1 on the Qxy axis, assigned to the crystalline OTS molecules and the (010) Bragg peak of the polymer, respectively.
2-133	Since the (010) diffraction peak was attributed to the π−π stacking of the polymer, this strong in-plane (010) diffraction peak indicates that the π−π stacking of the polymer is preferentially parallel to the substrates.[20,32]
2-134	Therefore, the GIXD patterns of PTDPPTFT4 suggest a highly ordered lamellar packing with an edge-on orientation.[17]
2-135	This edge-on molecular packing may facilitate charge carrier transport because either the π−π interchain stacking, the intrachain conjugation, or a combination of the two will be parallel to the direction of the drain current in the field-effect transistors.
2-136	The calculated lamellar spacing and π−π stacking of PTDPPTFT4 are 26.0 and 3.71 Å, respectively.
2-137	The lamellar spacing is consistent with an interdigitated packing for the side-chains.
2-138	The π−π stacking distance is comparable to other DPP-based
2-139	15,17,33−35
2-140	copolymers.
2-141	Charge transport of conjugated polymers is highly dependent on the intermolecular overlap integral.[36]
2-142	Therefore, this polymer, having a small π−π stacking distance, favorable to improve the intermolecular overlap integral, would be expected to show high charge carrier mobility.
2-143	To investigate the influence of temperature on the molecular orientation of PTDPPTFT4 films, the GIXD patterns of a PTDPPTFT4 film were measured.
2-144	Figure 6 shows the
2-145	diffraction peaks of the films annealed at different temperatures.
2-146	It was found that the diffraction intensity increased with annealing temperature, with a significant increase at 230 °C, while the full width at a half-maximum reduces when elevating the annealing temperatures.
2-147	This suggests that the crystallinity of polymer chains is significantly enhanced through thermal treatment.
2-148	To investigate the coherence length of the polymer orientation in the thin films, the full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the out-of-plane (200) peaks was calculated.
2-149	Figure 7 shows the correlation between the FWHM and
2-150	annealing temperature.
2-151	The films annealed at 230 °C showed a narrower FWHM (0.056 Å−1) for the Qz = (200) peaks, compared to that without thermal annealing (0.072 Å−1).
2-152	This suggests that the high annealing temperature reduces the orientation distribution of the polymer chains.
2-153	According to the Scherrer equation,[37] the out-of-plane coherence length (36.2 Å) of the polymer annealed in 230 °C is larger than that of the polymer without thermal annealing (26.7 Å), indicating the increase of the coherence length of the crystalline domain upon thermal treatment.
2-154	The increased annealing temperature may allow the movement of polymer chains, leading to better molecular ordering.[38]

2-166	Differences in degree of overlap of the orbitals of neighboring molecules could be a significant factor contributing to the differences in hole mobilities.
2-167	In polymeric materials, many factors can contribute to the enhancement of such orbital overlap, including coplanarity, extended conjugation, and π−π stacking.
2-168	Enhancements in these parameters lead to an increase in the transfer integral, which is a likely explanation for the increase in the hole mobility for FT4-DPP copolymers, as compared with FT4 polymers with no DPP component.
2-169	Accurate calculation of transfer integrals for a material requires precise information about packing, such as the crystal structure.
2-170	For polymers, where the crystal structure is not available, the ability to make such precise calculations is limited.
2-171	However, Transfer Integrals are generally enhanced by increasing planarity of the conjugated polymer backbone, which extends the conjugation field within a single chain, and increases the intermolecular π−π stacking.
2-172	It has been reported in the literature that intra molecular hydrogen bonds between oxygen atoms in the DPP units and β-hydrogen atoms of the neighboring thiophenes contribute significantly to the increase of the coplanarity, hence the intermolecular π−π stacking.[8]
2-173	Conformation analysis of TDPPTFT4 with the Accelrys Conformer Tool, based on the Compass force field, found the conformation where the doubly
2-174	bonded oxygen is facing a β-hydrogen to correspond to the lowest energy conformation.
2-175	To confirm this result, the structures with the oxygen atom facing a β-hydrogen and the oxygen atom facing a sulfur were considered, to further equilibrate their respective structures and compute their energies with Density Functional Theory (DFT).
2-176	For this study, DMol[3] program, another Accelrys module, was used with the double numerical plus d-functions basis set (DND) coupled with the PW91 exchange correlation function of the General Gradient Approximation (GGA).
2-177	It was found that the structure with the oxygen facing a β-hydrogen has the lowest energy, with higher ESP (partial atomic charges derived from ElectroStatic Potential fitting) absolute charge differences between O−H than O−S. These findings support the existence of intramolecular hydrogen bonds in thiophene substituted DPP species, which strengthen coplanarity of the main chain and promote strong π−π stacking.
2-178	Furthermore, the modeled HOMO and LUMO of PTDPPTFT4 (Figure 8) shows a skewed electron distribution
2-179	toward the DPP core, which promotes a strong interaction between the FT4 side of one chain and the DPP side of an adjacent chain.
2-180	The existence of such donor−acceptor regions in DPP-based repeat units has been reported to promote self- assembly of the polymer and hence increase charge trans- port.[6,39]
2-181	The existence of intramolecular hydrogen bonds that enhance the chain coplanarity in PTDPPTFT4 has been validated by DFT based calculations, which have revealed the nonsymmetric distribution of electrons.
2-182	These attributes, which contribute to the enhancement of the π−π stacking and hence the charge carrier mobility in PTDPPTFT4, are both absent in P2TDC17FT4 where no DPP is present.

3-002	A high performance polymeric thin film transistor semi- conductor material has been synthesized.
3-003	This polymer was characterized through spectroscopic and electrochemical methods to understand the band gap.
3-004	The long linear side-chains rendered this polymer easily synthesized and purified for potential scaling-up, because of appropriate solubility in the synthetic workup steps.
3-005	Variable temperature GPC character-
3-006	ization of this polymer indicates the formation of aggregates at temperatures below 200 °C. The molecular weight for nonaggregated polymer could only be obtained at an eluent temperature of 200 °C. The GPC characterization reported here for DPP based polymers indicates the importance for detailed studies to obtain molecular weight values representa- tive of the free polymer chains.
3-007	PTDPPTFT4 shows great solubility in a wide range of industrially friendly hydrocarbon solvents such as cyclooctane, xylene, toluene, and decahy- dronaphthalene.
3-008	GIXD and device studies have demonstrated the film quality and electrical performance.
3-009	Computational modeling has been used to help explain the structure property relationships contributing to high performance in organic thin film transistors.
3-010	This new polymer performs well in all the major areas necessary to be suitable for commercial applications, including synthesis, processability, stability, and electrical performance.
