1-002	Osteoporosis, characterized by the loss of bone mass and bone mineral density, is a worldwide health problem, particularly among postmenopausal women and elderly people.[1]
1-003	Osteopo- rosis is caused by disorders of bone homeostasis including excessive bone resorption by osteoclasts, and thus, several antiresorptive agents such as bisphosphonate and denosumab have been developed and are widely used for osteoporosis treatment.[2]
1-004	The antiresorptive agents currently in use efficiently inhibit osteoclastic bone resorption by reducing the number of osteoclasts.
1-005	However, it is difficult for patients to completely recover their bone health because bone remodeling is regulated
1-006	by a balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts and is occasionally hampered during treatment with antiresorptive agents.
1-007	Thus, a therapy for osteoporosis that inhibits bone resorption without affecting osteoclast viability is desired.
1-008	Recently, a vacuolar-type H+-ATPase (V-ATPase) in osteo- clasts was postulated as an attractive and a potential drug target
1-009	for osteoporosis therapeutics.[3]
1-010	The pumping of protons by the osteoclastic V-ATPase is a prerequisite for promoting bone resorption; therefore, specific inhibition of the osteoclastic V- ATPase without affecting the viability of osteoclasts would be a desirable mode of action in the treatment of osteolytic diseases.
1-011	Destruxin E (1), isolated from Metarhidium anisopliae by Paıs
1-012	et al. in 1981, is a 19-membered cyclodepsipeptide consisting of five amino acids (L-proline, L-isoleucine, N-methyl-L-valine, N- methyl-L-alanine, and β-alanine) and an epoxide-containing hydroxy acid derivative.[4]
1-013	It has recently been reported that
1-014	© 2013 American Chemical Society 296
1-015	destruxin derivatives exhibit potent V-ATPase inhibitory activity, and 1 in particular strongly inhibits the activity of V- ATPase.[5]
1-016	To date, the synthesis of destruxin and its derivatives has been reported by several research groups.[6]
1-017	We have recently achieved the first total synthesis and structural determination of 1 and also reported that the stereochemistry of the epoxide is crucial for potent V-ATPase inhibitory activity.[7]
1-018	In addition, destruxin E (1) intriguingly induces morphological changes in osteoclast-like multinuclear cells (OCLs) at low concentration without affecting the V-ATPase activity of the OCLs.[8]
1-019	Therefore, 1 is consequently considered as a new type of antiresorptive agent.
1-020	We are thus interested in the elucidation of the mode of action of destruxin E (1) in OCLs and its effect on bone metabolism in vivo.
1-021	However, the limited production of 1 from fungi such as M. anisopliae has prevented a detailed study of its in vivo mechanism.
1-022	Hence, the development of an efficient synthetic route to 1 is essential for obtaining sufficient quantities of 1 for in vivo studies.
1-023	We herein report the scalable solution-phase synthesis of the naturally occurring cyclodepsipeptide destruxin E (1) via a 2-methyl-6-nitrobenzoic anhydride (MNBA)-mediated macro- lactonization[9] and a diastereoselective dihydroxylation[10] of the terminal alkene in a 2-alkoxy-4-pentenoic amide using (DHQD)2PHAL as the chiral ligand and the L-proline moiety in the substrate as a chiral auxiliary.

2-002	To achieve the preparation of destruxin E (1) on a gram scale, we planned a solution-phase synthesis of 1, and the synthetic outline is illustrated in Figure 1.
2-003	The unstable epoxide side chain would be synthesized from macrolactone 2 in the final step.
2-004	The selection of the cyclization site was important for obtaining the desired macrolactone 2, and thus, the macro- lactonization at the β-Ala−α-hydroxy acid site was chosen in accordance with our previous sysnthesis.[6a,b,7] The cyclization precursor 3 would be obtained via coupling of acid 4 and tetrapeptide 5 followed by simultaneous deprotection of the N- and C-termini.
2-005	A TBS group was chosen to protect the hydroxyl group at the N-terminus of 3 and a 2-(trimethylsilyl)-ethyl (TMSEt) group was selected as the protecting group at the C-terminus of 5 because both the TBS and TMSEt groups can be readily removed by treatment with TBAF under ambient conditions without decomposition of the peptide sequence.[11]
2-006	The efficient preparation of 4 is a key issue for achieving the gram-scale synthesis of destruxin E (1).
2-007	The secondary alcohol at the 1γ position should be diastereoselectively prepared because the stereochemistry at the 1γ position is important for the inhibition of V-ATPase activity.[7]
2-008	We previously reported the synthesis of compound 4 from 8 through lactone 6; however, the total yield of 4 was not reproducible on a gram scale because of the high polarity of the resulting acid after hydrolysis of lactone 6a (Figure 2).
2-009	Moreover, the overall yield of the desired lactone 6a was less than 50% because of the non- stereoselective dihydroxylation of alkene 8, which was utilized to prepare both lactones 6a and 6b for structural determination of destruxin E (1).
2-010	Therefore, preparation of the key compound 4 utilizing lactone 6a is problematic on a gram scale.
2-011	Thus, an alternative route to 4 involving diastereoselective dihydrox- ylation of alkene 7 followed by acetal formation and removal of the benzyl group was anticipated to provide a higher yield.
2-012	In general, it is known that dihydroxylation of terminal alkenes proceeds with lower enantioselectivity than that of internal alkenes.[12]
2-013	Therefore, we expected that the proline moiety in 7 might act as a chiral auxiliary in the diastereoselective dihydroxylation of the terminal alkene moiety.
2-014	Compound 7
2-015	can be readily synthesized via the coupling of L-proline benzyl ester and the acid obtained by hydrolysis of the previously synthesized compound 8.
2-016	Compound 7 was initially synthesized from the previously reported[7] TBS ether 9 (Scheme 1).
2-017	In our previous report, the asymmetric allylation of 9 was performed using allyl bromide/ lithium hexamethyldisilazide (LiHMDS) at −30 °C for 16 h to achieve reproducible yield and diastereoselectivity.
2-018	To improve on these results, highly reactive allyl iodide was used as the electrophile.[13]
2-019	Expectedly, the allylation with NaHMDS at −45 °C was smoothly completed within 1.5 h, affording the desired product 8 in 94% yield with >95% diastereoselectivity.
2-020	However, acid 11 was not formed by direct hydrolysis of 8 under basic conditions because the cyclic carbamate was readily hydrolyzed under the reaction conditions.
2-021	The stepwise modification to give 11 through methyl ester 10 was then attempted via methanolysis of 8 utilizing samarium trifluor- omethanesulfonate.[14]
2-022	However, this reaction took over 48 h to go to completion, and the TBS group in 11 was easily removed under the acidic workup.
2-023	Thus, the nucleophilic cleavage of 8 with MeOMgBr was investigated;[15] this reaction proceeded successfully at room temperature to provide 10 in 94% yield without removal of the TBS group.
2-024	Hydrolysis of the methyl ester followed by amidation of the resulting acid 11 with L-Pro- OBn using bromotripyrrolidinophosphonium hexafluorophos-

2-026	phate (PyBroP)[16] then afforded the desired product 7 in 56% yield.
2-027	Once the gram-scale synthesis of the desired 7 was achieved, we next investigated the diastereoselective dihydroxylation of the terminal alkene in 7.
2-028	It is known that the asymmetric dihydroxylation of terminal alkenes generally proceeds with poor enantioselectivity.
2-029	Thus, extensive studies of the asymmetric dihydroxylation of terminal alkenes have been conducted, and bis(cinchona alkaloid) ligands with 1,4-phthalazine (PHAL) or pyrimidine (PYR) spacers have been shown to be effective for obtaining the corresponding diols with
2-030	high enantioselectivity.[12]
2-031	As an initial attempt at the synthesis of the desired diol 12a, the dihydroxylation of 7 utilizing AD- mix-β was examined (Table 1).
2-032	This reaction provided the desired diol 12a and its diastereomer 12b in a ratio of 67:33 (entry 1).[17]
2-033	By contrast, dihydroxylation utilizing AD-mix-α provided a 50:50 mixture of diastereomers (entry 2).
2-034	Thus, the dihydroxylation was further investigated using cinchonidine- based chiral ligands.
2-035	When the reaction was performed using (DHQD)2PHAL (5 mol %) in the presence of OsO4 (1 mol %), the diols 12 were formed in a 77:23 ratio (entry 3).
2-036	After extensive investigations, it was found that the dihydroxylation
2-037	of 7 utilizing K2 OsO2(OH)4−(DHQD)2PHAL quantitatively afforded 12a with 86% diastereoselectivity and that reduction of the osmium catalyst loading to 0.5 mol % did not affect the diastereoselectivity (entry 5).
2-038	On the other hand, it was found that with other chiral ligands, such as (DHQD)2 AQN[18] or (DHQD) 2 PYR,[12a] the yield or diastereoselectivity was decreased (entries 6 and 7).
2-039	Therefore, (DHQD)2PHAL was selected as the chiral ligand for the diastereoselective dihydroxylation of the terminal alkene in 7.
2-040	In contrast to the dihydroxylation of 7, the dihydroxylation of 8 under the optimized conditions utilizing (DHQD) 2PHAL and (DHQ)2PHAL resulted in poor stereoselectivity (6a:6b = 51:49 and 39:61, respectively; Scheme 2).
2-041	In addition, the dihydroxylation of methyl ester 10 in the presence of K2OsO2(OH)4−(DHQD)2PHAL afforded a mixture of lac- tones 6a and 6b in a 41:59 ratio.
2-042	Thus, it seemed that the chirality at the α-position in the hydroxyl acid derivative was mismatched in the dihydroxylation utilizing (DHQD)2PHAL for the induction of the desired stereochemistry at the 1γ position.
2-043	Therefore, the chirality of the proline moiety in 7 also would assist in the induction of the high diastereoselectivity obtained for 12a.
2-044	Because it was difficult to separate the resulting diols 12 using conventional column chromatography, acetal formation with 2,2-dimethoxypropane was performed on the mixture of diastereomers 12, and this was followed by isolation of the resulting acetal 13a via flash column chromatography in 66% overall yield.
2-045	Finally, removal of the benzyl group by hydrogenolysis furnished the desired compound 4 in 86% yield (Scheme 3).
2-046	With the desired 4 in hand, the gram-scale synthesis of the cyclization precursor 3 was then investigated.
2-047	Although we have reported the solid-phase synthesis of cyclization precursor 3 using an Fmoc method, we considered performing the gram-scale synthesis of 3 in solution because of facile scale-up and yield.
2-048	As it is well-known that peptide sequences including N- methylamide are readily hydrolyzed at the N-methylamide bond under acidic conditions,[19] peptide elongation utilizing the Boc strategy had to be avoided for the synthesis of 5.
2-049	Instead, the benzyloxycarbonyl (Cbz) group was selected as the protecting group.[6a,c−h] The Cbz group can be removed by hydrogenolysis under ambient conditions without cleavage of N-methylamide bonds, and Cbz-protected N-methylamino acids can be readily prepared in one step from the corresponding amino acids on a large scale.[20]
2-050	The starting material Cbz-β-Ala-OH (14) was thus converted to the corresponding TMSEt ester 1511b via condensation with 2-
2-051	(trimethylsilyl)ethanol.
2-052	Hydrogenolysis of 15 in isopropanol provided amine 16 without transesterification at the C-terminus.
2-053	The resulting amine 16 was then coupled with Cbz-NMeAla-OH using EDCI−HOBt, and dipeptide 17 was obtained in 86% yield.
2-054	However, when the preparation of 18 from 17 and Cbz-NMeVal-OH was carried out in the same manner as for the preparation of 17, the yield of 18 was moderate (56%).
2-055	After several attempts at the tripeptide synthesis, it was found that the use of HOAt instead of HOBt enabled efficient amidation to provide the desired product 18 in 90% yield without formation of the diketopiperazine through displacement of the C-terminal β-Ala residue.[6e,21] Next, tetrapeptide 5 was prepared through 19 in the same manner as used for 18, and then hexapeptide 20 was obtained via coupling of 5 with 4 using EDCI−HOAt.
2-056	Concomitant removal of the TBS and TMSEt groups with TBAF furnished the desired cyclization precursor 3 on a multigram scale (Scheme
2-057	4).
2-058	Next, we investigated the MNBA-mediated macrolactoniza- tion of 3 (Scheme 5).[8]
2-059	Previously, we performed the macrolactonization of 3 at 3 mM.
2-060	However, in the present study it was found that the macrocyclization of 3 could be achieved at 6 mM on a gram scale, providing the corresponding product 2 in 81% yield without formation of the dimerized byproduct (Scheme 5).
2-061	On the other hand, we also attempted the macrolactamization of L-Pro and L-Ile residues of 21 based on the previous reports.[6c,f,g] Although the macrolactamization of 2122 at 1 mM utilizing EDCI−HOAt proceeded smoothly at room temperature, a mixture of 2 and its dimer 22 in a 53:47 ratio was obtained (Scheme 6).
2-062	On the basis of previously reported conformational studies of destruxin A and roseotoxin B using NMR and X-ray crystallographic analyses, it has been suggested that the amido bond between N-MeAla and N-MeVal adopts the s-cis configuration, leading to a β-turn-shaped structure stabilized by two internal hydrogen bonds.[23]
2-063	There- fore, we believe that the cyclization precursor 3 forms a similar conformation as A, which is proper for the macrolactonization (Figure 3).
2-064	Wang et al.[24] recently reported that the destruxins are produced via nonribosomal peptide synthesis of a linear peptide, which undergoes macrolactonization in the biosysn- thesis.
2-065	Thus, the macrolactonization between β-Ala and the hydroxy acid would be structurally suitable for the ring construction of destruxins, rather than the macrolactamization between the proline and isoleucine residues.
2-066	With the desired macrolactone 2 on a gram scale, removal of the acetonide in 2 was performed [3 M aqueous HCl/dioxane (1:2), 10 °C, 10 min] in accordance with our previous report.
2-067	However, the yield of 26 was moderate because the N- methylamide bond was partially cleaved under the acidic conditions.
2-068	After further investigation, it was found that the ratio of water and dioxane was crucial for removal of the acetonide without cleavage of the peptide bond.
2-069	The reaction was complete under milder conditions [1.5 M aqueous HCl/dioxane (2:1), 0 °C, 2 h], providing diol 26 in 87% yield.
2-070	For completion of the scalable synthesis of 1, selective tosylation of the primary alcohol in 26 was carefully performed via treatment with 2 equiv of TsCl at room temperature to afford the desired tosylate 27 in 81% yield.
2-071	Finally, formation of the epoxide under basic conditions furnished destruxin E (1) in several hundred milligram yield with high purity (>98%) after silica gel column chromatography (Scheme 7).
3-001	In conclusion, we have accomplished the improved solution- phase synthesis of destruxin E (1) and established an efficient synthetic process for the production of a sufficient quantity of 1
3-002	in high purity for use in vivo experiments.
3-003	Diastereoselective dihydroxylation of the terminal alkene in 7 was successfully performed utilizing (DHQD)2PHAL, and the L-proline moiety
3-004	in the substrate was essential for inducing the high diastereoselectivity in this reaction.
3-005	It is also noteworthy that
3-006	the β-turn-shaped structure stabilized by internal hydrogen bonds and the s-cis configuration of the NMe amido bonds assisted in the efficient macrolactonization of 3 without high- dilution conditions, leading to macrolactone 2 without formation of the dimerized byproduct.
3-007	These improved synthetic processes made it possible to access a sufficient quantity of destruxin E (1) in high purity (>98%), and in vivo
3-008	experiments on 1 to study its effect on bone metabolism are currently underway in our laboratories.
