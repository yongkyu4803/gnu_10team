1-002	A recent analysis of the type of molecules within the pharmaceutical industry noted that there has been an increase in the proportion of “flat” molecules synthesized since the 1970s and that this correlates with the increased availability of chemistry facilitating sp[2]−sp[2] coupling.[1]
1-003	This trend has been driven, in particular, by the Suzuki reaction, which benefits from readily available and generally bench-stable boronates and boronic acids.
1-004	In addition, the widespread availability of palladium catalysts and ligands allow coupling of even the most challenging heterocyclic halides.[2−6]
1-005	Within the industry, there is now a great deal of interest in increasing the three-dimensional character of synthesized molecules, and straightforward methods to directly form sp 2−sp[3] carbon−carbon bonds have therefore become highly desirable.[7]
1-006	Although there have been significant recent advances in this field,[8−18] there are still several shortfalls in available methodologies.
1-007	One area that is particularly challenging is the coupling of saturated heterocycles such as oxetanes, azetidines and piperidines.
1-008	For example, data from the Pfizer electronic laboratory notebook shows that for aryl-alkyl couplings of azetidines and oxetanes, only one-third of such reactions worked in yields greater than 0%, with one-quarter giving yields in excess of 20%.
1-009	Because of the challenging nature of these reactions, sp[2]−sp[3] carbon−carbon bond construction in a pharmaceutical environ- ment has typically been performed in a stepwise manner, incorporating the more viable sp[2]−sp[2] coupling, followed by hydrogenation of the resulting alkene.[19,20]
1-010	(Scheme 1).
1-011	Clearly, a direct coupling to form the key bond is a more efficient approach, and there is significant interest in increasing the range of methodologies available to carry out this type of reaction.

1-013	Gonzalez-Bobes and co-workers recently reported a nickel- catalyzed Suzuki reaction with aliphatic halides (Scheme 2a).[17]
1-014	The reported yields were good to excellent, but the scope of the reaction was largely limited to alkyl or cycloalkyl substrates.
1-015	This reaction was applied to more pharmaceutically relevant substrates by Duncton et al., who report low to good yields (Scheme 2b).[18]
1-016	A range of aromatic and heterocyclic boronic acids were found to be synthetically useful in the reaction, but drawbacks included the requirement for a toxic and carcinogenic nickel catalyst and the high cost of iodinated starting materials.
1-017	In 2009, Barluenga et al. described a metal-free reductive coupling between tosylhydrazones and alkyl, alkenyl and aryl boronic acids (Scheme 2c) in good to excellent yields.[21]
1-018	This reaction bypassed the need for precious metal catalysts and highly air/moisture-sensitive or expensive coupling partners.
1-019	However, the substrate scope was primarily limited to benzylic, α-heterocyclic and/or aldehyde-derived tosylhydrazones, with lower yields observed for substrates that deviated from these limiting parameters.[21,22]
1-020	The scope was subsequently expanded by Nakagawa et al. (Scheme 2d), who applied the conditions to a protected 3-formylazetidine tosylhydrazone.[23]

3-001	In summary, we present a procedure for metal-free coupling between saturated sulfonylhydrazones and aromatic boronic acids.
3-002	This approach benefits from an electron-rich sulfonylhy- drazone and modified reaction conditions to enable cross- couplings of a range of 4-, 5-, and 6-membered saturated heterocycles with aryl and heteroaromatic boronic acid substrates.
3-003	The reaction tolerates functionality that is incompatible with many current methods and represents an improved procedure for the synthesis of these pharmaceutically relevant bicyclic building blocks.

4-002	General Experimental Methods.
4-003	All reactions were conducted using standard Schlenk techniques.
4-004	Analytical thin layer chromatog- raphy (TLC) was performed using silica gel 60 F254 precoated glass- backed plates and visualized by ultraviolet radiation (254 nm) and/or potassium permanganate as appropriate.
4-005	Flash column chromatog- raphy was performed using silica gel (particle size 40−63 nm) under air pressure.
4-006	1H NMR spectra were recorded on a 400 MHz spectrometer.
4-007	Chemical shifts are reported in ppm with the resonance resulting from incomplete deuteration of the solvent as the internal standard (CDCl3: 7.26 ppm, DMSO-d6: 2.50 ppm).
4-008	13C NMR spectra were recorded on a 100 MHz spectrometer with complete proton decoupling.
4-009	Chemical shifts are reported in ppm with the solvent resonance as the internal standard ( 13CDCl3: 77.0 ppm, t or 13C- DMSO-d6: 39.5 ppm, septet).
4-010	HRMS was performed using electro- spray ionization with time-of-flight mass analysis.
4-011	HRMS signals are reported to 4 decimal places and are within ±5 ppm of theoretical values.
4-012	Infrared spectra were recorded neat as thin films and only selected peaks are reported.
4-013	General Procedure A: Preparation of Sulfonylhydrazides 4b−4e.
4-014	Compounds were prepared according to a known procedure.[28]
4-015	To a solution of sulfonyl chloride (1.0 equiv) in THF (0.2 M) at 0 °C was added hydrazine hydrate (2.5 equiv) dropwise.
4-016	The reaction mixture was stirred at 0 °C until complete conversion was observed by TLC.
4-017	The mixture was diluted with EtOAc, washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4 and solvents removed in vacuo to give the title compounds.
