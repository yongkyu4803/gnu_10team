1-001	Hydrogen spillover, which has been observed in a variety of catalytic reactions, such as hydrogenation,[1,2] syngas reactions,[3,4] and hydrotreatment reaction,[5] among others, is a well-known phenomenon in catalysis.
1-002	The role of spillover was recognized in catalyst’s longevity and stability of activity and selectivity,[6] creation of catalytic centers,[7] and enhancement of reaction rate.[8]
1-003	Comprehensive reviews of spillover in catalysis have been published,[6,9-11] including those specific to hydrogen storage.[12,13]
1-004	Hydrogen spillover was applied in hydrogen storage in recent years by Lueking and Yang,[14] and appreciable enhance- ment of hydrogen storage by hydrogen spillover has been achieved by the following work.[15-18] Hydrogen spillover is a process composed of multiple steps, including catalyzed hydro- gen dissociation, surface diffusion from the catalyst to a surface that would otherwise be unable to adsorb dissociated hydrogen directly, possible chemisorption of the dissociated hydrogen species to the support, and recombination and subsequent desorption of mobile hydrogen species from the support.
1-005	(The generic term “species” is used, as the exact nature of spilt-over hydrogen is unclear in the literature, with H3 , Hþ ions, H atoms, and coexistence of Hþ and H atom possible depending upon the nature of the surface.[19]) The balance of these competing processes determines the overall hydrogen content on a surface.
1-006	As such, hydrogen spillover differs from simple physisorption or

1-008	chemisorption for which there is direct equilibration between the bulk gas phase and the active adsorption sites.
1-009	Although hydro- gen spillover includes both chemisorption and physisorption as steps in its mechanistic sequence, the process of surface diffusion effectively decouples the interaction between the gas phase and the active surface sites.
1-010	This decoupling leads to dependence on structural features that affect hydrogen supply to the surface (e.g., catalyst size, dispersion, interface, diffusion pathway) versus the probability of chemisorption (number of active surface sites; binding energy with hydrogen) versus the probability of recom- bination.
1-011	The premise in utilizing hydrogen spillover to enhance overall hydrogen uptake is that either the population of mobile hydrogen species is appreciable with continual resupply from the catalyst, or the dissociated active hydrogen species migrate to and are retained by surface sites to which they weakly chemisorb.
1-012	To improve overall uptake via hydrogen spillover, research has included investigation of interfacial contact between metal catalyst and support,[15,16] metal doping efficiencies,[20,21] structure of the support,[22,23] substitutional doping of the support with heteroatoms,[24-27] or altering of the surface chemistry of the
1-013	In catalysis, the reduction of a large number of metal oxides has been improved by hydrogen spillover.[28]
1-014	In particular, tung- sten and molybdenum oxide hydrogen bronzes have been thoroughly studied since Khoobiar’s initial experiments.[29]
1-015	It is also well documented that surface oxygen groups, specifically terminal hydroxyls, can facilitate hydrogen spillover to zeolites,[30] silica, and alumina.[31-33] The oxygen groups act to either stabilize the spilt-over hydrogen[30,34] or provide bridges by which H atoms can migrate over the support surface to occupy active sites previously inaccessible to them.[33]
1-016	However, the desorption of spilt-over hydrogen may occur via dehydroxylation of the sup- port in such cases.[30]
1-017	There is significantly less data on how surface functional groups on carbon-based supports will affect hydrogen spillover.
1-018	Wang et al. modified a carbon receptor with oxygen groups and found that the presence of oxygen groups increased the binding energies of the spilt-over H on graphite.[35]
1-019	Theoretical calculations showed increased hydrogen binding energy upon the oxidation of the carbon surface[13] and possible routes for decreasing diffusion barriers.[36]
1-020	The first objective of this study is to report the effect of added oxygen groups on hydrogen spillover, with emphasis on investigation of the chem- ical identity of specific surface oxygen functional groups.
1-021	To this end, we use a modified alkaline treatment to increase the number of oxygen functional groups on the surface and use multiple characterization techniques to understand the nature of the oxygen species active in accepting spilt-over hydrogen.
1-022	The low-temperature alkaline treatment, modified from Chiang et al.,[37] is expected to increase hydroxyl, carboxyl, and carbonyl groups on the surface of an activated carbon.
1-023	This work is a follow-up from a published conference proceeding from 2008.[38]
1-024	In preparation of this work and verification of the magnitude of these results, we have found interesting effects of trace water present during pretreatment.
1-025	The role of water, as will be discussed, lies in surface modification during pretreatment, rather than (or in addition to) directly affecting adsorption.
1-026	Although water is generally considered to be a detrimental contaminant to hydrogen storage,[39,40] water is also known as a necessary cocatalyst to activate hydrogen spillover,[41-43] and its presence during pretreatment and/or adsorption[11,33] may play a role in subsequent hydrogen spillover uptake in a process akin to the bridge-building process developed by Yang et al.[15,16] The second objective of the article is thus to explore the role of water, and its synergistic role in the development of surface chemistry and subsequent effect on hydrogen storage via the spillover phenomenon.
2-001	2.1.
2-002	Material Synthesis.
2-003	A high surface area activated carbon (AC, The Kansai Coke & Chemicals) was selected for additional surface modification.
2-004	This AC was originally prepared by alkali- metal activation of a graphitized petroleum coke.
2-005	To increase surface oxygen groups further, we treated the as-received AC with KOH, drawing from a procedure reported by Chiang et al.[37] The procedure was modified to use KOH rather than NaOH because KOH is widely used in the modification of carbon material for hydrogen storage.[22,44]
2-006	In brief, the AC was impreg- nated with 1 M potassium hydroxide (KOH, EMD Chemicals) in a KOH to AC weight ratio of 4:1, magnetically stirred for 12 h at room temperature, washed to neutral, filtered, then dried at 373 K. The resulting material after KOH activation will be referred to as AC-o.
2-007	The presence of oxygen groups is expected to affect the dispersion of the Pt catalyst, and thus secondary spillover studies were used to emphasize the role of surface chemistry without altering the “supply” of the hydrogen species from the catalyst.
2-008	The secondary carbon support (either AC or AC-o) was mixed with a Pt catalyst supported on activated carbon (5% Pt-C, STEM Chemicals) in a ratio of Pt-C to AC fixed at 1:9,[15] then ground with an agate mortar and pestle for 30 min (Pt-C/AC or Pt-C/AC-o).
2-009	In each series of experiments described below, samples within the series are from the same batch of synthesis.

2-011	Characterization.
2-012	BET specific surface area and total pore volume were calculated from nitrogen adsorption/desorp- tion isotherms at 77 K with Micromeritics ASAP 2020.
2-013	The micropore volumes were calculated from CO2 isotherms using the Dubinin-Astakhov (DA) equation.
2-014	Before both N2 and CO2 isotherms, the samples were outgassed at 2 mmHg for 6 h at 423 K.
2-015	The chemical bonding information of the modified surface was obtained by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Axis Ultra; Kratos).
2-016	The X-ray source for the measurement was monochromatic Al-KR.
2-017	The working pressure was maintained under 10-8 Torr. 80 eV pass energy was used for surveys, and 20 eV was for high-resolution scans.
2-018	For calibration purpose, the C (1s) electron bond energy was referred at 284.6 eV, which is corresponding to graphitic carbon.[45]
2-019	O-to-C atomic ratios were approximated from the XPS survey spectra by using the ratio of areas under the most intense photoelectron peak of each element.
2-020	Because the spot diameter of XPS is in millimeter scale, typically single spots were used.
2-021	Two different preparations of AC and AC-o were used (as noted below) in the quantification of the O/C ratios to demonstrate sample variability.
2-022	Samples were exposed to air prior to characterization.
2-023	We make the assumption that air exposure during sample transfer is expected to have minimal effect on the surface groups that remain chemically bound to the surface and could not be desorbed; our data show similar results regardless of time between synthesis/adsorption and characterization.
2-024	Diffuse reflectance infrared spectra (DRIFTS) were collected on a Magna-IR 750 Nicolet FTIR spectrometer in the range of 500-4000 cm-1.
2-025	All spectra were collected at 50 °C. Samples were exposed to air prior to characterization.

3-125	The pro- nounced low-pressure hydrogen uptake is observed for oxidized samples only when trace water is found in pretreatment (i.e., Setup 1 and wPt-C/AC-o in Setup 2).

3-134	In general, the number of oxygen groups on a carbon surface is expected to decrease with heating in an inert gas,[52,62] and carboxyl groups (-COO) correlated to CO2 evolution are expected to evolve at temperatures as low as 150 °C.[52] However, the evolution/modification of surface-functional groups is more complicated when treated in H2 .

3-150	3.4.
3-151	Role of Oxygen Groups in Hydrogen Spillover: Pro- posed Mechanisms.
3-152	We show that increased oxygen groups, particularly of carbonyl/quinone type, correlate with increased low-pressure hydrogen spillover.
3-153	Several experimental[35,38] and theoretical[36] studies that have attempted to increase the number of oxygen groups on spillover materials reported that adding surface oxygen can improve hydrogen spillover and stabilize
3-154	19,35,63,64
3-155	spilt-over hydrogen.
3-156	In particular, a recent paper by Yang et al. showed that the oxygen groups, especially semiqui- none group, increased the reversible hydrogen storage capacity significantly.[65]
3-157	However, the role of oxygen groups in hydrogen spillover needs further study; explanations for this effect can be categorized as follows: (I) bridge-building, (II) surface binding and chemical kinetics, and (III) oxidation and reduction.
3-158	In terms of (I), surface oxygen groups may provide diffusion paths, in a method analogous to a “bridging”[42] or a “brigade”.[11]
3-159	Theoreti- cally, adjacent oxygen groups increase binding energies between hydrogen and carbon surfaces[35] as well as bind directly to semiquinone or lactone groups.[65]
3-160	Hydroxyl groups or ethers may lower the energy path for diffusion.[36]
3-161	In terms of (II), Lueking et al.[46] showed that increased binding energy leads to increased surface coverage using a Monte Carlo analysis of the kinetics of this spillover reaction sequence assuming a “loosely bound” hydrogen spillover state, as demonstrated experimentally by Mitchell et al. via inelastic neutron scattering.[59]
3-162	Hydrogen species bound to the surface with sufficient energy could account for the ∼1 wt % residual chemisorbed hydrogen found as surface hydroxyls in postadsorption XPS characterization.
3-163	In terms of (III), oxidation (either the KOH treatment or the subsequent “wet” pretreatment) is expected to modify the electronic proper- ties of the surface.
3-164	Oxidation-reduction reactions on carbon
3-165	surfaces have been reviewed,[66] but the process is complicated by not only the type of carbon and oxygen groups but also their proximity and ability to stabilize any introduced charges.
3-166	More characterization is needed to verify this idea.
3-167	3.5.
3-168	Optimization of Surface Functional Groups for Spil- lover.
3-169	Here we characterize but do not necessarily control or adapt synthesis to give preferential formation to a particular type of oxygen group.
3-170	Yang et al. recently suggested that semiquinone groups are the ideal receptor of spilt-over hydrogen because lactone groups will lead to irreversible hydrogen adsorption because of stronger binding energy.[65]
3-171	Psofogiannakis et al’s[36] simulation showed that desorption of spilt-over hydrogen bound to oxygen functional groups desorbs more favorably as water rather than molecular hydrogen.
3-172	If true, this would have obvious negative consequences for the reversibility of hydrogen storage in oxygen-modified carbon materials.
3-173	Whether the hydrogen that remains chemically bound to the surface after pressure reduction to 12 mbar will desorb as H2 or H2O will be the subject of a subsequent paper.
3-174	We anticipate that this will depend on the nature of the oxygen functional groups, and different oxidation methods may bring about different oxygen groups.
3-175	Further experimental work includes temperature-programmed desorp- tion experiments on various oxygen functional groups to identify desorbed species and check the formation of water on the surface.
3-176	For now, the increase in the amount of readily desorbable hydrogen with oxidation (0.25 wt % for Pt-C/AC-o vs 0.06% for Pt-C/AC) seems to support hypothesis II.
4-001	In the current study, XPS characterization results show that KOH treatment produces oxygen groups on an activated carbon sample with a large fraction being quinone type groups.
4-002	The oxidation treatment has a negligible effect on physisorption but leads to a significant enhancement of hydrogen adsorption in the presence of a hydrogen-dissociation catalyst, that is, hydrogen spillover.
4-003	Gravimetric adsorption measurements show that hy- drogen spillover to the oxidized surface is pronounced at low pressures (P T ratio of 444 and a seven-fold increase in overall hydrogen uptake relative to its constituents.
4-004	The presence of water during pretreatment is necessary to realize this low-pressure enhancement, and signifi- cant hydrogen spillover was not observed when scientific grade hydrogen was used during pretreatment.
4-005	We speculate that this is due to increased oxygen functional groups formed during pre- treatment in the presence of water, active carbon sites formed via water reaction on the carbon surface, or both.
4-006	The mechanism for the water-assisted pretreatment needs further clarification, as does its alignment with several proposed mechanisms on the effect of surface chemistry on hydrogen spillover.
4-007	The pro- nounced low-pressure effect agrees with other publications[50,67] but is expected to be highly dependent on the nature of the sample.
4-008	The current results indicate that oxidation of the surface of the support will facilitate low-pressure, ambient-temperature hydrogen spillover but may not necessarily lead to hydrogen that readily desorbs at ambient temperature.
4-009	XPS and FTIR surface characterization after H2 desorption suggests that strongly bound hydrogen forms hydroxyls with introduced functional groups; there is only a vague indication of new carbon-hydrogen bonds.
4-010	A certain fraction of hydrogen is found to be loosely bound in that it readily desorbs with pressure reduction and cannot be accounted for by physisorption to the carbon materials.
4-011	However, the state of this loosely bound hydrogen cannot be determined without in situ methods.
4-012	On-going work is looking at the effect of surface sites on the temperature of desorption and on the nature of the desorbed species.
4-013	The lack of hydrogen desorption without heating leads to practical engineering hurdles for hydrogen storage and also highlights that both adsorption and desorption isotherms should be considered in the evaluation of modified carbon materials.
4-014	The results also show that the oxidation of carbon species to facilitate hydrogen spillover is dependent on both the amount and the nature of surface oxygen species.
