1-002	Conjugated polymers have been used as the active layers in organic electronic devices, such as light emitting diodes (OLEDs), photovoltaics (OPVs) and transistors (OTFTs), due to their unique optoelectronic properties and solution processability.[1−4] The optoelectronic properties and device performance are determined not only by the intrinsic chemical nature of the conjugated polymers[5,6] but also by the preparation condition of the active layers[7,8] It is known that the microstructures of conjugated polymers in the solution and thin-film states are highly sensitive to the processing

1-004	polymer with another flexible polymer, which may be called the “co-electrospinning”.[27]
1-005	For instance, the fabrication of poly(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) nanofibers has been accomplished by using a two-component core−shell system, where the P3HT and the flexible polymer (such as PEO and PMMA) formed the core and shell, respectively.[20−26]
1-006	Polyfluorenes constitute a family of conjugated polymers exhibiting a number of attractive features for LED application.
1-007	Poly(9,9-dioctylfluorene-2,7-diyl) (PF8) is one of the most important members in this family, as a blue-emitting materi- al.[28−31] The optoelectronic properties of PF8 were found to depend strongly on its molecular packing and conformation.

1-014	Chen et al. showed that PF8 exhibited the crystalline α-phase with a helical conformation,[33] while the mesomorphic β-phase was charac- terized by a larger intrachain torsional angle, which resulted in a more extended conformation and a longer conjugation length (as schematically illustrated in Figure 1).[34]
1-015	The structural characteristic of the β-phase is favorable for promoting the carrier mobility of the optoelectronic devices;[35,36] therefore, controlling the content of β-phase in PFs is an important task for enhancing the device performance.
1-016	Recently, Kuo et al. have successfully prepared the electrospun nanofibers of polyfluorene derivatives using their binary blends with PMMA.[28]
1-017	They showed the presence of discontinuous fibril-like domains in the PF8 fibers at low PF8/ PMMA blending ratio, but a continuous core−shell structure was formed at high PF8 content.
1-018	The higher photo- luminescence (PL) efficiency in electrospun fibers was attributed to the much smaller PF8 aggregate domains in the fibers than those in the corresponding thin films.
1-019	Yang et al. reported that a well-dispersed conjugated polymer in an optical matrix manifested dramatically improved photophysical proper- ties by a stress effect because the individual molecular strands were fully stretched in the diluent sample.[12]
1-020	Indeed, many studies have indicated that the strong stretching forces in an electrospinning process may induce orientation of polymer chains along the long axis of the fiber.[37,38]
1-021	Such a stretching effect could also contribute to the enhancement of the PL efficiency.
1-022	Therefore, the relationship between the conforma- tional order and the photophysical properties in the electrospun fibers is of great interest for controlling or enhancing the optoelectronic performance of conjugated polymers.
1-023	However, the influence of the microstructure development on the PL
1-024	efficiency of PF8 in the electrospun fibers is still largely unexplored.
1-025	In this study, we report the direct electrosponning of PF8 fibers without resorting to the blending with a flexible homopolymer.
1-026	More importantly, we demonstrate that the conformational structure characterized by the intrachain torsional angle in the PF8 backbone is affected significantly by the dragging force associated with the electrospinning by showing the formation of a significant amount of β-phase (in which the polymer chains are more extended) in the as-spun fibers.
1-027	Using the combination of temperature-dependent wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) and PL spectroscopy, we further examine the transformation of the microstructure induced by heating and its corresponding effect on the PL efficiency.
1-028	The result may provide the fundamental under- standing on modulating the photophysical properties of electrospun PF8 fibers by thermal treatment.

2-002	Materials and Solution Preparation.
2-003	Poly(9,9-dioctyl- fluorene-2,7-diyl) (MW approximately 65000g/mol) was purchased from American Dye Source, Inc.
2-004	(ADS129BE).
2-005	High-purity chloroform (HPLC grade) was used to prepare conjugated polymer solutions.
2-006	The solutions were prepared by stirring appropriate quantities of PF8 in chloroform at approximately 60 °C for 2 h until macroscopically homoge- neous solutions were observed by the naked eyes.
2-007	The solutions were used for electrospinning to prepare the PF8 as-spun (AS) fibers and for obtaining the films by drop-casting.
2-008	Electrospinning and Film Casting.
2-009	PF8/chloroform solutions with the concentration of 20.0 wt % were used to prepare the AS fibers by electrospinning.
2-010	The electrospinning setup included a Glassman PS/FC60P high-voltage power
2-011	supply.
2-012	During electrospinning, a positive high voltage was applied via a copper wire to the spinning solution inside a glass pipet with a capillary tip with the diameter of 0.21 mm.
2-013	An electrically grounded ITO glass slide or a stainless steel net were placed approximately 30 cm from the pipet tip.
2-014	The PF8 solution flow rate was set to 0.4 mL/h.
2-015	As the applied voltage was increased to 30 kV, a solution jet was created.
2-016	The jet followed a complex stretching and looping path as it dried and solidified to form AS fibers.
2-017	During spinning, the PF8 chains could be more extended as the average intrachain torsional angle along the PF backbones may be increased by the dragging force.
2-018	The AS fibers in the mat had a very high aspect ratio, and so few fiber ends were identifiable.
2-019	The drop-cast PF8 thin films were prepared by dropping the solutions onto a microscope coverslip at ca. 30 °C under a purge of nitrogen gas.
2-020	Morphological Characterizations.
2-021	The morphology of PF8 AS fibers was investigated by polarized optical microscopy (POM) and fluorescence optical microscopy (FOM) using a Zeiss Axioskop-40 optical microscope (OM).
2-022	The detailed morphology of the PF8 as-spun fibers was observed with a Hitachi S-4700 field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) operated at an accelerating voltage of 1.0 kV.
2-023	Spectroscopic Measurements.
2-024	The absorption measure- ments for the π−π* and β-phase absorption of PF8 were performed using a Hitachi U-3010 spectrophotometer.
2-025	The fiber mats, approximately 100 μm thick, were placed on the surface of ITO glass slides for UV−vis and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy measurement.
2-026	The UV−vis spectra were corrected for the ITO glass background.
2-027	The PL spectra were recorded with a HMTECH MFS-630 Multi spectrophotometer, using an excitation wavelength of 235 nm.
2-028	The temperature-dependent PL spectra were obtained by heating the specimens using a Linkam TH600 heating stag under nitrogen atmosphere at a rate of 10 °C/min.
2-029	Thermal Properties.
2-030	The thermal transitions of PF8 AS fibers and thin films were detected using a Perkin-Elmer Pyris Diamond differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) equipped with an intracooler.
2-031	The samples of approximately 3 mg in weight were placed into the sample pans and heated under nitrogen to 280 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min.
2-032	The samples were then annealed at 280 °C for 5 min to eliminate the thermal history followed by cooling to 30 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min to detect the thermal transitions during cooling.
2-033	Microstructure Characterization.
2-034	The transformation of the microstructure of the PF8 AS fibers induced by heating was investigated by the temperature-dependent WAXS experiment conducted at Beamine BL17B3 of the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (NSRRC), Taiwan.
2-035	The X-ray beam with the wavelength (λ) of 1.33 Å was collimated into a 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm beam size.
2-036	The WAXS profiles over the scatting vector (q = 4π sin(θ/2)/λ, with θ being the scattering angle) range of 0.8 to 48 nm−1 were collected under the sample-to- detector distance of 0.307 m.
2-037	The detector used was an imaging plate (Fuji BAS III, area = 20 × 40 cm2 ) having 100 μm pixel resolution.
2-038	The scattering intensities were corrected for the absorption and the air scattering.
2-039	The temperature-dependent scattering profiles were probed in situ from 30 to 180 °C at a heating rate of 2.0 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere.

4-001	■
4-002	We provided insight into the influence of the thermally induced microstructure development on the photoluminescence efficiency (Ep) of PF8 fibers prepared by direct electrospinning without resorting to polymer blending.
4-003	Comparing with the cast film, PF8 AS fibers exhibited broad spectra, with a red-shift in UV−vis absorption and a blue-shift in PL, respectively.
4-004	The microstructure of AS fibers is heterogeneous, composed of the amorphous phase and the β-phase as the major and minor component, respectively.
4-005	We deconvoluted the WAXS profiles to calculate the fractions of different types of microstructure at various temperatures.
4-006	We found that the microstructure development of PF8 fibers was complex as the temperature was raised.
4-007	Four main features were identified: (1) there was a minor fraction of β-phase within the AS fibers that remained essentially unperturbed at temperatures below Tg; (2) the fraction of the β-phase decreased progressively with the increase of temperature above Tg, and accompanied with such a decrease is the increase of the fraction of the γ- or α′-phase (i.e., Cγ-/Cα′-crystals) at temperatures between Tg and Tct; (3) the Cγ-/Cα′-crystals transformed into the more stable Cα-crystals over Tct; (4) above Tm, the crystals were disrupted into the disordered molten state.
4-008	We demonstrated that the Ep was directly influenced by the microstructure of the PF8 fibers.
4-009	Ep decreased slightly at T g and then dropped markedly at Tg cc; however, a significant increase of the Ep was observed near Tcc before it decreased again with further heating up to 200 °C.
