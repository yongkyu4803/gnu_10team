1-001	■
1-002	Supercapacitors have emerged as a highly promising device technology for temporary electrical storage, thanks to intrinsi- cally high power densities that enable fast charging and discharging as well as excellent cycling behavior.
1-003	Ideal supercapacitor electrode materials should exhibit high electrical conductivity, high specific surface area, and area-specific capacitance, as well as good mechanical strength, yet be chemically inert to ensure long device lifetime.
1-004	For these reasons, sp[2] carbon materials have attracted a great deal of attention.[1−4] Nevertheless, limitations in the energy density of carbon-based supercapacitor electrodes remain a significant impediment.[5]
1-005	Besides development of advanced electrolytes, efforts toward increased capacitance have mostly been focused on optimizing the overall electrode morphology in order to increase the surface area and to take advantage of recently discovered pore size effects.[2,6−8] The underlying notion is that energy storage nominally occurs in the electric double layer present at the electrode−electrolyte interface and that the capacitance is therefore proportional to the active contact area.
1-006	Furthermore, the capacitance of nanopores can depend strongly on the pore size; for example, it has been shown to increase 3-fold as the pores become smaller than the size of the solvated ion.[7]
1-007	However, it is important to emphasize that a strategy based exclusively on increased electrode−electrolyte contact area and pore morphology neglects the crucial role played by the electronic structure of the carbon electrode itself.
1-008	© 2013 American Chemical Society
1-009	The importance of electronic structure in determining the area-specific capacitance of an electrode/electrolyte interface has been suggested in the past.[9,10]
1-010	It is also an accepted concept in the field of electrochemically gated graphene-based field- effect transistor devices.[11]
1-011	However, in the field of electro- chemical supercapacitors, the effect of quantum capacitance on device performance has only recently begun to attract attention.[12−14] This is despite its increasing relevance as high surface area synthesis techniques improve and traditional morphological limitations are overcome.[15−18] As an example, a recently synthesized graphene-derived electrode demonstra- ted an active, electrolyte-accessible surface area approximating that of a mass-equivalent graphene sheet, with both top and bottom electrode surfaces in contact with the electrolyte solution.[5,19]
1-012	Nevertheless, the area-specific capacitance was shown to decrease with increasing active surface areain direct conflict with predictions based solely on electrode morphol- ogy.[4,5]
1-013	On the basis of these and similar results, it is clear that a new improvement strategy that directly accounts for the intrinsic electronic structure of the electrode is required.
1-014	This paper uses first-principles calculations on a variety of model systems in order to explore the nature and origin of intrinsic limitations in the capacitance of graphene-derived carbon electrodes as well as to suggest strategies for

1-019	We show that the capacitance limitation of graphitic carbon-based electrodes is indeed traceable to low electrode quantum capacitance as the limit of single-layer, graphene-like local morphology is approached, which directly illustrates the lack of available states for carriers to occupy upon charging.
1-020	More significantly, we show that the limiting low density of states, and thus the capacitance, can be enhanced by tuning the local defect structure, morphology, and thickness of a carbon-based electrode.
1-021	By investigating and evaluating each of these possibilities independently, we attempt to provide a theory-inspired roadmap that may be used to devise coherent design strategies for improving the capacitance in graphene-derived electrode materials.

2-003	Ultrasoft pseudopotentials[21] were employed with plane-wave and charge-density cutoffs of 30 and 300 Ry, respectively.

2-006	For the rippled surfaces, 4 × 4 hexagonal supercells (32 carbon atoms) were used.
2-007	Rippling was induced by decreasing the in-plane lattice parameter perpendicular to the zigzag direction, convoluting the out-of-plane coordinates with a sinusoidal variation, and relaxing the resulting structure within the compressed unit cell.
2-008	The folded geometry was produced in a similar manner, using an orthorhombic 2√3 × 9 supercell (8 atoms across, 104 atoms total).
2-009	The cell was compressed parallel to the zigzag direction and relaxed after convolving with a sinusoidal variation.
2-010	The degree of compression was chosen such that the spacing between the folds matched the experimental interlayer spacing in graphite.
2-011	For all other surfaces, including in-plane strain and thickness calculations, the primitive cell was used.
2-012	The interplanar spacing of the multilayer samples was fixed to the experimental value.
2-013	Except where indicated, all ionic degrees of freedom were relaxed to determine equilibrium structures.
2-014	Relaxations were performed using a 12 × 12 in-plane k-point mesh for the primitive cell, which was increased to 96 × 96 for accurate determination of the density of states for the final configuration.
2-015	For larger supercells, this same k-point mesh density was retained.
2-016	A Gaussian electronic smearing of 0.007 Ry was applied to aid k-point convergence.
2-017	For calculations under a fixed bias potential, the effective screening medium (ESM) method[23] was employed.
3-001	Definition of Capacitance.
3-002	The total capacitance of an electrode−electrolyte interface can be modeled as an equivalent series circuit of component capacitance contributions from the solid electrode (Cs ) and the liquid electrolyte (Cl, itself consisting of Helmholtz and diffuse regions).[9,24]
3-003	This series representation is used routinely in fields such as graphene-based transistor devices.[11]
3-004	Here, we apply it to supercapacitors, following a similar framework to that used in refs 12−14.
3-005	Within this model, the contributions sum is
3-006	Ctotal−1 = Cs−1 + Cl−1 (1)
3-007	from which it is clear that the smaller of the two component terms will limit the overall device capacitance.
3-008	Note that this construct assumes that Cs and C l can be treated independently, whereas in practice, they demonstrate a complex and ill- understood interdependency.[24]
3-009	Nevertheless, we adopt this model as a useful first-order approximation for the purpose of devising improved electrode optimization strategies.
3-010	For a perfect screening medium such as an ideal metal, the intrinsic electrode capacitance is effectively infinite and does not play a role in determining the device capacitance, and excess charge remains confined to the surface.
3-011	However, in carbon materials, perfect screening is not attainable.
3-012	In this case, the electrode capacitance Cs is represented by the quantum capacitance Cq, which can be evaluated directly from the electronic density of states (DOS) at a given voltage V:

3-015	The above definition assumes the voltage V is referenced to the zero-bias Fermi level, i.e., the neutral (uncharged) electrode potential.
3-016	However, for supercapacitor applications, the most important limiting quantity is usually the total energy storage capacity.
3-017	This quantity is based not on the differential capacitance, but rather on its value integrated over a complete charge/discharge cycle, assuming equilibrium conditions (i.e., slow charge/discharge).

3-019	One can think of the integrated quantum capacitance in eq 3 as the effective storage capacity per unit applied potential.
3-020	Because we are concerned primarily with providing guidelines for enhancing the energy density of graphene-based super- capacitors, this is the preferred definition used in this paper.
3-021	We caution that the integrated quantum capacitance depends on the potential reference, which in eq 3 is assumed to be the Fermi level of the neutral working electrode, rather than a universal fixed-reference potential or counterelectrode.
3-022	Neglect- ing pseudocapacitive contributions, this level can be considered a reasonable approximation of the potential at the point of zero charge (PZC).
3-023	As such, the definition given in eq 3 is probably best compared with the half-cell voltage in a two-electrode symmetric configuration.

3-027	For instance, cyclic voltammetry and galvanostatic measurements can be used to derive the integrated or differential capacitance, whereas impedance spectroscopy is used to assess the differential capacitance.[26]

3-076	Thickness Dependence and Screening Behavior.
3-077	The experimentally observed decrease in area-specific capacitance near the single-layer limit[5] suggests that excess charge induced on the surface of a graphene substrate may not be effectively screened within the electrode.
3-078	This relates to the low concentration of surface states for pristine single-layer graphene, as shown in Figure 1.
3-079	In this event, there may be a benefit to using multilayer graphene structural units to realize higher area-specific quantum capacitance, as the additional layers can contribute to the image charge.
3-080	Additional layers could be especially valuable when both faces of the electrode are simultaneously exposed to the electrolyte, as in most electrode designs.
3-081	It is possible to perform a straightforward fixed-band quantum capacitance calculation for multilayer graphene, as done in eq 1 for single-layer graphene.
3-082	However, use of the fixed-band approximation to assess capacitance becomes highly suspect in the case of multilayer samples.
3-083	To see why, one must consider the effect the presence of the electrolyte has on the electrochemically measured quantum capacitance.
3-084	The electro- lyte causes the formation of an electric double layer (EDL) at the interface with the electrode, which induces an electric field that decays into the electrode.
3-085	For single-layer samples, all atoms are assumed to be in contact with the electrolyte, meaning the interfacial electric field shifts the electrode potential essentially uniformly.
3-086	In this case, the fixed-band approximation remains reasonable to within a constant potential shift.
3-087	However, in multilayer electrodes, only the outer layers are in direct contact with the electrolyte.
3-088	As a result, the outer layers see a different potential from the inner layers, with the degree of the difference determined by the screening properties of the electrode.
3-089	In this event, the EDL- induced potential shift is no longer uniform throughout the electrode, and the standard formulation of the fixed-band approximation (which is not spatially resolved) breaks down.
3-090	Given the intimate connection between the quantum capacitance limitation and the EDL screening properties, it is useful to gain an actual measure of the extent of the screening length.
3-091	From this, we can derive the minimum thickness for near-complete screening of an interfacial double layer and consequently of maximal area-specific capacitance.
3-092	To do so, we evaluate the electrode screening properties as a function of layer thickness using the effective screening medium (ESM) method.[23]
3-093	The ESM method modifies the Hartree potential via a Green’s function formalism to simulate insertion of the electrode slab between two virtual media with specified dielectric constants.
3-094	We polarize the slab by applying a fixed electric field of 0.4 V/Å between the two virtual infinite- dielectric perfect metals.
3-095	This is comparable to the interfacial field strength generated by the EDL.
3-096	The decay of the polarization response into the interior of the multilayer electrode slab is then used to obtain a measure of the Debye−Huckel screening length.
3-097	thickness.
3-098	We use ABA stacking for all samples.
3-099	It is immediately obvious that for thinner slabs the polarization response does not fully decay within the electrode.
3-100	This is a manifestation of the low density of surface states, which forces displaced charge to the inner electrode layers.
3-101	Beginning with the six-layer slab, screening behavior improves significantly.
3-102	For the thicker slabs, the decay of the polarization response can be fit to an exponential curve.
3-103	For a field strength of 0.4 V/Å, the 1/e screening length is found to be 2.2 Å.
3-104	To obtain a 95% screening of the polarization response therefore requires 6.6 Å of material, which corresponds to about two additional layers beyond the surface.
3-105	This should be doubled for supercapacitor operation in which the both surfaces are assumed to be in contact with the electrolyte, meaning graphitic electrodes should be at least five or six layers thick in order to generate the highest area-specific capacitance.
3-106	However, for actual device engineering, this must be mediated by considerations of gravimetric and volumetric capacity, which will decrease with thicker samples.
3-107	We point out that the screening and DOS limitations of single-layer graphene will be similarly relevant for engineering electrodes derived from other sp[2] carbon derivatives.
3-108	For instance, supercapacitors based on single-walled carbon nano- tubes (SWCNTs) would be expected to screen more poorly than few-layer multiwalled nanotubes (MWCNTs).
3-109	This intrinsic limitation may be partially responsible for the higher observed capacitances of MWCNTs with respect to SWCNTs.[38]
3-110	The fact that successive layers in multilayer graphene experience a different potential under actual operation due to the presence of the electrolyte, as we have supposed, is borne out clearly in the ESM calculations of Figure 2.

3-112	approach allows us to evaluate the validity of the fixed-band approximation for calculating the quantum capacitance of multilayer slabs.
3-113	In doing so, we find that the fixed-band approximation fails even for bilayer slabs and more spectacularly for thicker slabs.
3-114	This can be seen in Figure 3,
3-115	which plots the band structure of n-layer graphene for n = 1, 2, and 8 before and after application of the electric polarization field.
3-116	In order to permit direct comparison, we have aligned the zero of energy to the Fermi level in the absence of the electric field.
3-117	For single-layer graphene, the band structure does not change upon application of the polarizing field, suggesting the fixed-band approximation holds well within the voltage range of interest.
3-118	However, for bilayer and 8-layer graphene, the presence of the field breaks the symmetry and induces level splitting in the states nearest the Fermi level.
3-119	For bilayer graphene, this opens a gap near the K point, as reported previously.[39−41] For 8-layer graphene, the field-induced level splitting instead creates a near-continuum of bands.
3-120	Interest- ingly, for the thicker slab, there is also a contribution to the density of states from the π* states, which are driven lower in energy by the field until they mix with the states nearer the Fermi level.
3-121	Although this shift of the π* states is also seen for the thinner samples, its magnitude depends on the thickness (it is barely visible beyond E − EF = 2.0 eV for the bilayer).
3-122	It is worth noting that there is a corresponding shift of the π states upward toward the Fermi level; however, the degree of the shift is much smaller and does not appear within the ±2 V window even for the 8-layer slab.
3-123	A similar asymmetric π−π* field response has been reported in recent experiments on bilayer graphene.[40,41]
3-124	We emphasize that the rehybridization of states near the Fermi level for thicker slabs in Figure 3 would not be captured in the fixed-band approximation, nor would the observed level splitting at the K point for multilayer slabs.
3-125	Moreover, the response is asymmetric, with the π* states apparently much more sensitive to an applied field than the π states.
3-126	As such, detailed studies of the quantum capacitance of multilayer slabs should be done using more direct methods rather than relying on the fixed-band approximation.
3-127	We are currently investigating this in detail.
3-128	On the other hand, single-layer graphene can be adequately described within the fixed-band approximation for the voltage range of interest, at least for field strengths on the order of 0.4 eV/Å.
3-129	Naturally, the magnitudes of the level shifts in Figure 3, as well as the observed screening length, should depend on the field strength.
3-130	The dependence is likely quasi- linear, matching the behavior reported for screening radius in
3-131	the basal plane.[42]
3-132	In this regard, the fixed-band approximation could fail even for single-layer graphene at large field strengths.
3-133	However, for most realistically achievable field strengths, this is unlikely to be the case.
3-134	As such, we continue to rely on the fixed-band approximation for the structures in the remainder of this paper, all of which are derived from single-layer geometries.

3-136	Several experimental measurements of the capacitance of single-layer graphene have exhibited significant deviations from the zero-temperature ideal limit and from the pristine graphene behavior shown in Figure

3-139	Common features include a significantly enhanced, nonzero density of states at the Fermi level, and enhanced capacitance at potentials close to zero bias that is largely flat across a range of tens or hundreds of meV.
3-140	This leads to better- than-predicted capacitive behavior in this region, which authors have generally attributed to additional states due to defects or local electronic density oscillations.
3-141	In other words, the electronic signature of graphene implies that it is unlikely to retain a zero-temperature, defect-free structure under actual testing conditions.
3-142	It is reasonable to assume that this effect will be amplified in intrinsically defect-rich systems, such as polymer-derived, three-dimensional graphene structures.[19]
3-143	Selective and deliberate inclusion of certain defects through controlled synthesis may therefore be used as a tool to enhance the quantum capacitance.
3-144	Accordingly, we devote the following sections to a detailed analysis of the effects of local modifications to graphene-based materials.

3-159	The degree to which this is the case depends both on the defect species and on the applied potential; however, in nearly all instances, the effect is to increase Cqint with respect to the graphene baseline.
3-160	The 55−77 Stone−Wales defect is the only point defect we tested that did not appreciably change the density of states at the Fermi level itself; nevertheless, it does increase the observed Cqint at nonzero bias in both positive and negative directions.
3-161	This agrees with previous DOS calcu- lations.[45−47]
3-162	Particularly large contributions near the Fermi level are observed for the 555−777 divacancy and for the 5−9 monovacancy in the presence of hydrogen or nitrogen, resembling a pseudocapacitive feature.
3-163	In fact, the capacitance profile of these three look very similar across the voltage range in Figure 5, suggesting resemblance in their electronic structure.
3-164	Closer analysis of the DOS revelas that in each of these cases a common predominant feature in the electronic structure is the formation of a new quasi-localized state at the Fermi level, which is half occupied at zero bias.
3-165	Examination of the projected DOS shows that this state is derived from the pz orbitals of carbon atoms immediately surrounding the vacancy, where the dangling bonds and nonhexagonal rings exist.
3-166	We suggest that it results from a discontinuity in the π manifold due to the missing atom(s), which breaks the local symmetry and results in a disconnected, half-filled pz state that is higher in energy.
3-167	We point out that the especially large peak in the hydrogen- and nitrogen-saturated monovacancy in Figure 5 resembles calculations of hydrogen adsorption on pristine graphene.[45]
3-168	Chemisorption of hydrogen or nitrogen could therefore contribute significantly to quantum capacitance in graphene derivatives with high concentrations of exposed edges or pores.
3-169	Indeed, chemical modification could represent a valuable engineering strategy; we are currently pursuing this research direction by testing other chemical functional groups.
3-170	In addition, the profile of the 5−9 monovacancy in the presence of oxygen strongly resembles that of the 5−8−5 divacancy.
3-171	A new, largely pz-derived state is also created in these cases, although it is fully unoccupied rather than half- occupied at zero bias.
3-172	This shifts the effective minimum in the integrated quantum capacitance toward negative voltages.
3-173	The difference between the oxygen- and hydrogen/nitrogen- terminated monovacancies is probably a consequence of the strong electron-withdrawing nature of the oxygen at the monovacancy edge, which depletes the charge in the six- membered ring to which it is attached.
3-174	The hydrogen- and oxygen-terminated monovacancies also feature some distortion of the carbon−hydrogen and carbon− oxygen bonds out of the graphene basal plane, likely because the vacancy size is too small to fully accommodate the adsorbate.
3-175	This distortion, which is accompanied by a slight degree of sp[3] rehybridization, may serve to further enhance the symmetry-breaking of the π manifold that leads to the localized pz-derived state.

3-177	This is because we fix the supercell dimensions to the lattice parameter of pristine graphene.
3-178	However, our estimates suggest that these contributions are quite small compared to the observed increases (see Table S1, Supporting Information) and that the defect chemistry produces the dominant effect.

3-201	Local Strain and Curvature.
3-202	Since the physical morphol- ogy of the electrode surface represents an additional possible variable in the processing of nanoporous carbon substrates, we also ran a series of simulations investigating the capacitance of curved and strained graphene surfaces.[39]
3-203	The effects of in-plane strain were tested as well as surface buckling under compression.
3-204	The changes in fixed-band capacitance upon application of high uniaxial strain applied parallel to the zigzag direction, and upon application of biaxial strain applied uniformly along both planar directions, are shown in Figure 7.
3-205	Linear expansions of the equilibrium in-plane lattice parameter by 5% and 10% were examined (for biaxial strain, these were applied equivalently in both in-plane directions).
3-206	We also tested the case of biaxial compression based on a 5% linear contraction in the lattice parameter, under the constraint of no out-of-plane atomic rearrangement.
3-207	Although useful for examining capacitance trends, the magnitude of these strains is much higher than what is typically achievable experimentally for pristine graphene (generally <2%).[54−56] Still, the surface variations in nanoporous carbon, and the nonequilibrium process by which they are produced, are likely to generate local regions with above- average strain energy.
3-208	We also point out that much higher graphene strains of 10−20% have recently been reported for templated structures.[57,58]
3-209	The results in Figure 7 show a general trend of increased area-specific Cqint with in-plane tensile strain.
3-210	This is true even after accounting for the correspondingly enhanced surface area.
3-211	(The gravimetric Cqint would show an even more dramatic effect than that shown in Figure 7.) For the range of ±1.5 V, the changes scale with voltage and with the level of applied strain.
3-212	This agrees with previous calculations of reduced Fermi velocity in strained graphene.[59]
3-213	The increase in Cqint for biaxial
3-214	expansion of the lattice parameter by 5% is approximately equal to the 10% uniaxial expansion case.
3-215	Since these translate to essentially equivalent surface areas, this suggests that the directionality of the applied strain is less important than the magnitude of the areal increase in predicting capacitance.
3-216	The dependence between areal strain and capacitance is nearly linear at small voltages, as shown in the inset of Figure 7.
3-217	It arises from a flattening of the π/π* bands with strain, which effectively shifts these bands closer to the Fermi level.[60]
3-218	According to Figure 7, in-plane compressive strain tends to dampen Cqint with respect to pristine graphene.
3-219	However, under compression, a graphene surface is known to corrugate in order to dissipate strain energy.[61]
3-220	To simulate such corrugation, we compressed the cell along the zigzag direction and allowed for sinusoidal variation of the out-of-plane coordinates.
3-221	Four different values of in-plane compression were explored (5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% linear compression), corresponding to different characteristic wavelengths λ of the sinusoidal surface ripples: λ = 8.1, 7.7, 7.3, and 6.8 Å.
3-222	The resulting changes in Cqint are shown in Figure 8.
3-223	The calculations demonstrate that a large enhancement of the quantum capacitance can be realized upon rippling and that this enhancement scales with curvature (inversely with wavelength).
3-224	Note that the contribution to the capacitance is opposite in sign from what we saw for in-plane compression.
3-225	However, application of compressive stress is much more likely to induce surface buckling than in-plane compressive strain, since the former is a lower-energy transformation.
3-226	Having already established that tensile stress also enhances capacitance, we therefore conclude that applied stress of either sign is likely to be linked to increased quantum capacitance.
3-227	Accordingly, electrodes should be engineered to maximize local variations in morphology in order to increase local stresses and stress gradients; planar geometries are inefficient by comparison.
3-228	This is probably a contributing factor in the experimentally reported capacitance enhancement of graphene with curved morphologies.[17]
3-229	Since even pristine graphene is known to exhibit corrugation by room-temperature activation of low-frequency modes,[39] it is reasonable to assume some degree of capacitive enhancement of the type seen in Figure 8 should be present natively.
3-230	As such, one should expect somewhat more aggressive scaling of Cqint with voltage magnitude than is predicted for perfect, unstrained graphene.
3-231	This could be a contributing factor in the theoretical underprediction of the measured Fermi velocity of graphene.

4-001	■
4-002	In conclusion, we have introduced and evaluated strategies for improving the capacitance of graphene-derived supercapacitors that are based on modifying the local structure and morphology of the electrode rather than global surface area and pore geometry.
4-003	To do so, we have calculated the quantum capacitance within the fixed-band approximation of a variety of defective graphene-based surfaces containing point defects as well as local strain and curvature.
4-004	Our calculations confirm that the interfacial capacitance of graphene-derived electrodes is generally limited by the quantum capacitance, which is a consequence of the low density of states near the Fermi level.
4-005	However, the addition of defects into the graphene structure can significantly improve the calculated capacitance if introduced in sufficient concentrations.
4-006	For instance, the presence of hydrogen at vacancy-derived nanopores can increase the capacitance near zero bias significantly, whereas Stone−Wales defects have a much larger effect at higher voltage magnitudes.
4-007	These could prove an important tool for increasing graphene-based supercapacitor performance.
4-008	Similarly, tensile strain and rippling upon compression tend to increase capacitance at higher voltage magnitudes.
4-009	Folding the graphene sheet also increases capacitance at positive potentials.
4-010	Heavily curved local morphologies, which introduce rippling, folding, and regions of local tensile strain, should therefore be desirable over planar geometries.
4-011	An in-depth analysis of the electrode screening properties reveals that multilayer regions are far more effective at screening the double-layer potential than are single-layer regions, which leads to higher area-specific capacitance.
4-012	In this regard, five graphene layers ought to be sufficient to expect nearly full screening.
4-013	However, the thickness consideration should be weighed against the importance of gravimetric capacitance, which decreases with thickness.
4-014	Our analysis shows that the fixed-band approximation, in which the density of states is assumed to be rigidly shifted with changes in electronic occupation, remains reliable for single-layer samples but breaks down for multilayer samples due to level splitting and rehybridization of the π* states.
4-015	It should be noted that our calculations do not account for the interdependence of the electrode and electrolyte contributions to the capacitance or for the electrode stability; a more detailed study to this effect is currently under way.
