1-001	■
1-002	In recent years, the major greenhouse gas, carbon dioxide, has been proposed a practical hydrogen storage material.[1]
1-003	The selective photocatalytic reduction of CO2 to hydrogenated products containing a carbon has been the subject of considerable attention over the ages as a means for a carbon capture and light energy storage strategy.
1-004	Various molecular metal complexes based on Ru, Rh, Ni, Fe, Mo, Ti, and Ir have demonstrated hydrogenation of carbon dioxide to formic acid.[2,3]
1-005	Most catalysts showing a high turnover frequency for CO 2 reduction were used under supercritical conditions due to the solubility problem of carbon dioxide.
1-006	Only a few molecular metal catalysts were used in water.
1-007	Although most of transition- metal-hydride species were known to be unstable, an isolated ruthenium-hydride species was reported for the reduction of CO 2.[4,5]
1-008	In the study of the isolated ruthenium-hydride species, the Ru−H bond was inserted by CO 2 to produce formate.
1-009	On the other hand, the decomposition of formic acid/formate in the presence of homogeneous metal catalysts has also been studied to generate hydrogen and carbon dioxide.[6]
1-010	Although the decomposition of formic acid using molecular metal complexes has been poorly studied before, several reports in this area have been published recently.
1-011	Most studies with molecular metal catalysts reported the non-photochemical decomposition of formic acid, and often use thermal energy to achieve high conversion rates and yields.
1-012	However, examples of the photochemical system with such metal complexes are rare in the decomposition studies of formic acid/formate.

1-015	examples of the photochemical system containing a molecular metal catalyst for the HCOOH decomposition are required to understand the photocatalytic mechanism of formic acid decomposition and develop an efficient catalytic system utilizing formate/formic acid as one of the major sources of hydrogen.
1-016	Natural cofactor 1,4-dihydronicotinamide adenine dinucleo- tide (NADH) is regenerated in natural photosynthesis and used for many NADH-dependent enzymes to carry out various catalytic oxidation and reduction reactions in biological systems.
1-017	Therefore, NADH regeneration is considered as another means for solar-to-chemical energy storage.
1-018	Reduction of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) to the enzymati- cally active NADH has been carried out using chemical, enzymatic, electrochemical, and photochemical methods.
1-019	Enzymatic NADH regeneration methods have been studied extensively using formate dehydrogenase for various applica- tions.[7,8]
1-020	Although platinum nanoparticles (PtNPs) and molecular cobalt complexes photochemically regenerated NADH,[9,10] cyclopentadienyl-based Rh complexes have been frequently studied as catalysts to develop new electrochemical and photochemical systems for NADH regeneration and understand their reaction mechanisms.

1-031	A number of photocatalytic systems for hydrogen production using various molecular metal complexes have been developed over the past few decades.[15−20] Metal-hydride species have been proposed as reactive intermediates in most proposed reaction mechanisms for hydrogen photoproduction.
1-032	An organometallic Ir complex chemically reduces NAD+ to NADH by consuming H2, and an iridium-hydride intermediate was proposed as a reactive intermediate.[21]
1-033	In another study, a iridium-hydride intermediate, chemically generated by aliphatic alcohols, was proposed to react with a proton and NAD+ to generate hydrogen and NADH, respectively.[22]
1-034	Recently, a homogeneous system comprising [RhIII(dmbpy)2 Cl 2]Cl (dmbpy = 4,4′-dimethyl-2,2′-bipyridine) and Ru(bpy)32+ was reported for a visible-light-driven hydrogen production, but a Rh−H intermediate was proposed to chemically react with a proton and produce hydrogen.[17]
1-035	Further studies are needed for a complete elucidation of the properties of metal-hydride species especially in photocatalytic hydrogen production systems.
1-036	To the best of our knowledge, no reports are available regarding a catalytic hydrogen production by molecular Rh complexes and formate in the presence of Pt nanoparticles activated with visible-light energy.
1-037	Recently, PtNPs have been reported to mediate photo- chemical NADH regeneration without any other photo- sensitizers upon visible-light irradiation.[10]
1-038	In the photocatalytic NADH regeneration with PtNPs, no hydrogen production was observed, showing the preference for NAD+ reduction.
1-039	In other reports, Pt and Fe nanoparticles also mediated NADH decomposition in the presence of an organic chromophore as a photosensitizer to produce hydrogen.[23]
1-040	In the NADH oxidation, 9-mesityl-10-methylacridium (Acr-Mes) upon acti- vation with visible light catalyzes the oxidation of NADH and mediates the electron transfer to PtNPs during hydrogen photoproduction.
1-041	A photochemical study using molecular Co catalysts showed that the presence of colloidal Pt improves photocatalytic hydrogen production yield and rate.[20]
1-042	Thus, more research will be needed to completely elucidate the role of PtNPs, especially in the activation of metal-hydride species, for developing efficient hydrogen and NADH photoproduction systems.
1-043	Here, we report the visible-light-driven photoproduction of hydrogen by molecular Rh complexes and formic acid/formate in the presence of PtNPs in aqueous solution.

2-002	The chemical regeneration of NADH was performed with 0.13 mM [Cp*Rh(bpy)(H2O)]2+ (1) and 0.4 M sodium formate, affording >95% based on initial NAD+ in pH 7.0 phosphate buffer, similar to results reported previously (Scheme 1).[14]
2-003	However, about 40% NADH was generated in 50 mM phthalic acid buffer (pH 4.5):CH3CN (1:1), which was the solvent condition used in the photoreaction for hydrogen production in this study (Supporting Information (SI) Figure S1).
2-004	A solution containing 1, formate, NAD+, PtNPs (5 ± 1 nm, spherical; see the Supporting Information), and eosin Y (eosin) was irradiated with visible light (420 nm cutoff filter) in phthalic acid buffer/CH3 CN to test the reactivity of PtNPs and eosin for visible-light-driven photoproduction of hydrogen using the chemically in situ generated NADH.
2-005	Under these conditions, hydrogen corresponding to 78 turnover numbers (TN) of 1 was produced after 3 h of irradiation (Table 1, run 1).
2-006	Assuming that one hydrogen molecule was produced from a molecule of NADH, the amount of hydrogen produced corresponded to 10 turnovers of NAD+, demonstrating that NADH was regenerated during photocatalytic hydrogen production.
2-007	In the absence of 1 or 1/formate, H2 was barely observed due to no formation of RhIII−H (runs 2 and 4).
2-008	These data also demonstrated that formate without the Rh complex was not activated in the presence of PtNPs and eosin to produce hydrogen.
2-009	It was reported that a system containing only Acr-Mes and PtNPs afforded H2 using NADH under stoichiometric conditions upon visible-light irradiation.[23]
2-010	The H2 photoproduction resulted from the chemical regeneration of NADH and subsequent reduction of the protons on PtNPs utilizing visible-light-driven activation of eosin and the reducing power of NADH (Scheme 2, pathway I), similar to the NADH/ Acr-Mes/PtNPs reaction system.[23]
2-011	The photoreaction was performed in the absence of NAD+ to understand additional electron and proton sources for hydro- gen production in this system.
2-012	Interestingly, we observed 5.1 μmol of H2 (run 5), indicating that NADH is not the sole electron/proton source for H 2 photoproduction.
2-013	It was proposed that a rhodium(III)-hydride species was generated as a reactive intermediate in the reaction of 1 and formate.[13]
2-014	The preceding results demonstrate that the in situ generated RhIII −H species can transfer their electrons and proton to PtNPs upon photoactivation to produce H2.
2-015	Indeed, control
2-016	experiments showed that the additional elimination of 1 afforded no significant formation of H2 under the same conditions (run 6).
2-017	Such a significant difference in the hydrogen production in the absence (20 TN) and presence (78 TN) of NAD+ demonstrates that the hydride transfer reaction of RhIII−H to NAD+ (pathway I) was more favorable than to PtNPs (pathway II) under the run 1 conditions (Scheme 2).
2-018	Pathway II in which the rhodium(III)-hydride species transferring electrons to the photoactivated PtNPs for hydrogen production was first observed in this study.
2-019	Without any light, no hydrogen was observed in pathway II.
2-020	In pathway II conditions (containing only 1, formate, and PtNPs), also no significant hydrogen was produced in the elimination of PtNPs.
2-021	These data demonstrate that PtNPs were activated upon irradiation to abstract electrons from the RhIII−H species and the Rh−H was not directly activated by visible light for hydrogen production.
2-022	Fukuzumi and co-workers reported the hydrogen production by 1 with HCOOH with no irradiation, but a high concentration of 1 and a continuous addition of HNO 3 at a lower pH were used to produce hydrogen.[14]
2-023	When 1 and HCOOH were reacted in the dark under our reaction conditions, no hydrogen was observed over 8 h.
2-024	The apparent quantum yields (QY) of 1.8 and 0.6% for hydrogen production were obtained in pathways I and II, respectively.
2-025	Such low QY derived from the dependence of hydrogen production on NADH in pathway I and Rh−H in pathway II, which were generated during the catalytic reactions.
2-026	The low QY due to the low stability of NAD+ and 1 during the catalytic cycles would be improved by developing more stable substitutes of them.
2-027	The effects of eosin to pathways I and II were examined to understand the role of eosin in H2 production.
2-028	After 3 h irradiation in the absence of eosin, runs 3 and 7 afforded 12 and 24 TN of hydrogen, respectively.
2-029	In pathway I, the absence of eosin decreased the yield sharply (run 3), indicating that the NADH activation for hydrogen production on PtNPs required the photoexcitation of eosin, similar to other reports.[23]
2-030	To understand pathway I further, we separately carried out a photoreaction of NADH (Aldrich) in the presence of only PtNPs in phthalic acid buffer:CH 3CN.
2-031	No significant production of hydrogen was observed during photolysis of a solution of NADH and PtNPs, demonstrating that r4 is almost zero in the absence of eosin and that PtNPs alone had no photocatalytic activity for hydrogen production under the conditions.
2-032	These data also hinted that the 12 TN hydrogen produced in run 3 mostly derived from pathway II, and NADH generation (r3) and hydrogen production (r4) competed for RhIII −H species.
2-033	Based on these results, eosin and PtNPs worked cooperatively in pathway I to oxidize NADH and produce hydrogen upon visible-light activation.
2-034	In contrast, H 2 production in pathway II increased in the absence of eosin, demonstrating that eosin interfered with H2 production (run 7 vs run 5).
2-035	Indeed, the H 2 production rate
2-036	decreased progressively with increasing eosin (Figure 2a).
2-037	As eosin containing a carboxy functional group competes with
2-038	formate for binding to the Rh center in the r1 step, the RhIII−H formation rate slowed down in the presence of eosin.
2-039	Indeed, the H2 production rate progressively decreased with eosin (SI Figure S2).
2-040	In a control experiment, no hydrogen was observed in the absence of PtNPs (run 8), demonstrating that PtNPs were a main component for hydrogen production.
2-041	It was also reported that PtNPs alone had a reducing power upon irradiation of visible light in NAD+ reduction.[10]
2-042	In pathway II, PtNPs were the only species to activate RhIII−H for electron transfer.
2-043	These results also indicate that the hydrogen obtained in run 3 mostly derived from pathway II.
2-044	Due to the different effects of eosin on pathways I and II under the run 1 conditions, the eosin concentration dependence on the H2 production rate showed that the rate increased with increasing eosin until 0.15 mM; then it started decreasing (Figure 2b).
2-045	These results imply that pathways I and II contributed together to the H2 production rates until 0.15 mM eosin, but the contribution of pathway II decreases progressively with increasing eosin as shown in Figure 2a.
2-046	When eosin was
2-047	above 0.15 mM, pathway I became much superior to pathway II.
2-048	In pathway II with no eosin, the presence of 1.0 mM NAD+ reduced the amount of hydrogen almost by half (run 3), relative to that without NAD+ (run 7).
2-049	In the presence of NAD+, NADH generation (r3) only competed with hydrogen production (r5), as shown in Scheme 1.
2-050	Indeed, in the study of NAD+ concentration dependence on hydrogen production, the progressive decrease in H 2 production rate was accompanied by a progressive NADH increase at 1 h with increasing NAD+ (Figure 3).
2-051	Based on the reduction potentials of NAD+ and H+
2-052	under the conditions of 25 °C and pH 7.0, the hydride transfer from RhIII−H to NAD+ was expected to be more favorable than to a proton (Scheme 3).[24]
2-053	The NAD+ reduction was also faster
2-054	than a proton reduction by cobalt(III)-hydride species generated upon visible-light activation of eosin.[9]
2-055	Therefore, the additional presence of NAD+ to the pathway II condition resulted in consuming the RhIII −H needed for the electron and proton transfer to PtNPs and, accordingly, decreased the H2 production rate.
2-056	As shown earlier, the chemically generated rhodium(III) hydride was capable of reducing NAD+ as well as reducing H+ upon photoactivation of PtNPs.
2-057	We compared hydrogen and NADH production by 1 to those by 2 and 3 which contain the electron-donating methoxy and electron-withdrawing carboxy substituents in bipyridine, respectively (Figure 1), to examine the effect of nucleophilicity of RhIII−H on hydrogen production rates and to understand the rate-determining steps in the two pathways.
2-058	Under the run 1 conditions (pathway I), the electron-donating substituent resulted in a higher rate of H2 production (Figure 4a).
2-059	The resulting negative ρ value (−0.64) demonstrated that the nucleophilic reaction of NAD+ reduction (r3) among r1−r4 was rate-determining because the r1 and r2
2-060	steps are electrophilic and r4 is independent of the properties of Rh catalysts.
2-061	Furthermore, chemical reduction of NAD+ to NADH using 1 and formate in H2O/tetrahydrofuran showed the same rate-limiting step[25] and the nucleophilic protonation step of Ir−H species was also rate-limiting in the chemical production of H2 using formic acid.[26]
2-062	We also carried out chemical reduction of NAD+ using 1−3 and formate.
2-063	The rates obtained from the increase in the NADH absorption band at 340 nm showed a linear correlation of the log plot and also afforded a similar negative value (−0.64, SI Figure S3), consistent with the preceding conclusion.
2-064	However, under the conditions of pathway II without NAD+ and eosin, introducing an electron-withdrawing substituent afforded a higher H2 production rate and a positive ρ value (0.66; Figure 4a).
2-065	These results suggest that the r2 step of the RhIII−H formation was rate-limiting because the r5 step would be, in nucleophilic terms, similar to the reduction of NAD+.
2-066	Such a positive ρ value in a Hammett plot also appeared in the Pd−H formation by the elimination of O2 from Pd-OOH species.[27]
2-067	In order to clarify the reaction mechanisms of pathways I and II, the rates of r2 and r3 were compared by plotting the initial rates (kobs) vs formate (Figure 4b).
2-068	The rate of r2 was obtained by observing the chemical NADH regeneration using 1, formate, and NAD+.
2-069	The rate of r3 was obtained by observing the H2 production under the conditions of pathways II (run 7).
2-070	These results showed that the rate of r3 was much slower than that of r2 and corresponded to the results of the Hammett study shown previously.
2-071	Deuterium kinetic isotope effects were investigated with 1 and DCOOH in pathways I and II (Figure 5).
2-072	In pathway I, the
2-073	rate of hydrogen production by DCOOH/DCOONa became much slower than that by HCOOH/HCOONa (Figure 5a).
2-074	The kinetic isotope effect based on the initial rates turned out to be 4.7, similar to 4.3 obtained in the chemical NADH regeneration using 1 and DCOOH/DCOONa (SI Figure S4).
2-075	These results also supported that the NADH generation step was rate-limiting in pathway I, as described previously.
2-076	Pathway II was also influenced by DCOOH/DCOONa (Figure 5b).
2-077	However, the kinetic isotope effect appeared to be 2.1 which derived from the difference in the generation step (r2) of RhIII−H and RhIII −D from RhIII−OC(O)H and RhIII−OC(O)D, respectively.
2-078	These results agreed with that obtained in the
2-079	Hammett study shown previously.
2-080	These normal kinetic isotope effects observed in pathways I and II corresponded to that obtained with [Cp*Rh(bpy)(H2O)]2+ and HCOOH/DCOOH in the chemical hydrogen production[14] but were in contrast to the inverse kinetic isotope effect observed with Acr- Mes/PtNPs and HCOOH/DCOOH in the absence of a Rh catalyst.[23]
2-081	The inverse kinetic isotope effect was interpreted to result from the zero-point energy of the Pt−H and Pt−D bonds on PtNPs which was generated by a proton-coupled electron transfer from the photoexcited Acr-Mes to PtNPs.
2-082	If the hydrogen production from Pt−H generated by such a fast proton-coupled electron transfer step from Rh−H to PtNPs would have been rate-determining in pathway II, the similar inverse isotope effect should have been observed.
2-083	To clarify the mechanism further, more research remains to be done.
2-084	The Rh(III)/Rh(I) reduction potential of 1−3 changed systematically with the electronic nature of the substituents (Figure 6).
2-085	The reduction peak potentials at pH 4.5 occurred at
2-086	−644, −722, and −632 mV vs Ag/AgCl for 1, 2, and 3, respectively.
2-087	A close relationship existed between the reduction potentials and the electron-donating or -withdrawing characters of the substituents (SI Figure S5).
2-088	The order of the reduction potentials was well associated with those of the hydrogen photoproduction reactivity of 1−3 in pathway I which included the nucleophilic rate-limiting step associated with Rh−H species.
2-089	This conclusion well corresponded with what was shown in the Hammett study (Figure 4a).
2-090	This indicates that the electron-donating methoxy group more readily allows for hydride transfer of RhIII−H to NAD+.
2-091	Based on all data presented previously, the RhIII−H species was clearly the main component to produce NADH in pathway I and transfer electrons to PtNPs in pathway II.
2-092	While the RhIII −H species chemically reduced NAD+, they produced hydrogen only upon irradiation of PtNPs.
2-093	In pathway I for hydrogen production, the chemically regenerated NADH was oxidized by the photoexcited eosin finally to transfer a proton and electrons to PtNPs (Figure 7).
2-094	On the other hand, in pathway II, PtNPs activated by visible-light energy abstracted electrons from RhIII −H. The fluorescence of eosin was not quenched by NADH, but quenched by PtNPs.
2-095	These
2-096	observations indicated that an electron was transferred from the photoexcited eosin to PtNPs and then NADH replenished an electron to eosin+..
2-097	However, through the overall process of pathway II, the rate-determining step turned out to be the formation of the Rh−H species from the rhodium-formate adduct based on Hammett and kinetic studies.
3-001	Hydrogen was efficiently generated in a visible-light-driven photocatalytic system consisting of NAD+, Rh catalyst, eosin Y, sodium formate, and PtNPs in an aqueous solution.
3-002	NADH chemically produced from the Rh catalyst 1 and sodium formate was consecutively consumed by photoactivated eosin Y and PtNPs to produce hydrogen using visible-light energy.
3-003	In addition, the rhodium-hydride species generated in the reaction of 1 and formate was proposed to reduce a proton in the presence of photoactivated PtNPs.
3-004	Two distinct pathways for hydrogen production were observed and divided by NAD+ and proton reduction including RhIII−H species.
3-005	Interestingly, PtNPs should be activated by visible light to carry out hydrogen production from the rhodium(III)-hydride species.
3-006	Based on kinetic studies, the NADH formation step was rate-limiting in the H2 production pathway through NADH (pathway I), whereas the rate-determining step in pathway II was the generation of RhIII−H. In pathway I, the photoexcited eosin Y shuttled electrons from NADH to PtNPs to produce hydrogen.
3-007	In pathway II, the RhIII−H species directly transferred their electrons to photoexcited PtNPs upon irradiation.
3-008	The different rate-limiting steps were evidenced by the Hammett plots using different Rh complexes and the kinetic isotope effect using DCOO−/DCOOH.
3-009	This study has provided important insight into the mechanism of the photocatalytic hydrogen production from formate/formic acid using molecular metal complexes and Pt nanoparticles.
3-010	The conversion of light energy into stored chemical energy, such as formic acid and NADH, is on an interesting subject for the development of artificial photocatalytic systems.
3-011	The develop- ment of efficient photocatalytic systems for the hydrogen production from formic acid and NADH based on such a detailed mechanistic study will influence on practical applications for hydrogen storage and hydrogen production systems.
