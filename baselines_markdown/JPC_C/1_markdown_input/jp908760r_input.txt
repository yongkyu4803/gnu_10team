1-001	Dye-sensitized solar cells (DSCs) offer a promising solution to the urgent need for a cheap source of renewable energy.[1,2]
1-002	A typical DSC is designed as an electrochemical cell consisting of a mesoporous network of an electron-transporting material, usually interconnected TiO2 nanoparticles, sensitized with light- absorbing dye molecules and infiltrated with a redox-active electrolyte or solid-state hole transporter.[3]
1-003	This film is contacted on both sides with electrodes to complete the device.
1-004	Light absorption in the dye results in rapid electron injection from the dye excited state into the TiO2 conduction band. 4 These electrons are then transported through the TiO2 matrix via diffusion and are finally collected at the transparent conducting anode.
1-005	Meanwhile, the oxidized dye molecules are regenerated by electrons donated from the electrolyte or hole transporter, and the positive charge is transported to the cathode, completing the circuit.[5,6]
1-006	To date, efficiencies of 11.2% and 5.1% have been achieved using a redox-active liquid electrolyte and a solid organic hole transporter (spiro-OMeTAD) as the hole trans- porter, respectively.[7,8]
1-007	However, these values are still much lower than the 25% efficiency achieved using first-generation single-crystal silicon solar cells, 9 and significant effort is being invested to reduce this gap, in order to improve the commercial viability of DSCs.
1-008	The electron transport mechanism in the TiO2 mesoporous network is a critical step that affects overall device performance.
1-009	Over the past few decades, a large amount of theoretical and experimental work has been carried out to allow a better understanding of electron injection into and movement through the semiconductor matrix toward the electrode, with the aim of increasing the charge generation and collection efficiency.[10–22]
1-010	However, most of the current understanding of the electron
1-011	dynamics in TiO2 is based on indirect estimates derived from the application of electron-transport models.
1-012	These include various modifications of the Drude model,[13,23] effective medium theory,[15] or Monte Carlo[24,25] or FTDT simulations.[26]
1-013	Time- resolved microwave conductivity (TRMC) measurements offer a useful photoconductivity probe but can only achieve nano- second temporal resolution.[16]
1-014	In the present study, we have used optical-pump terahertz-probe (OPTP) spectroscopy to measure the change in photoconductivity in TiO2 as a function of probe delay with respect to an excitation pulse, in the subpicosecond to nanosecond time range.
1-015	Being an all-optical technique, OPTP is an excellent probe for measuring charge dynamics in nanostructured materials,[27,28] avoiding any artifacts arising from electronic contact to the mesoporous semiconductor film.
1-016	Additionally, unlike most photocurrent measurement techniques, OPTP experiments can be conducted on bare TiO 2, without the need to fill the film with an electrolyte in order to complete the circuit.
1-017	Thus, this technique allows us to probe the intrinsic (or local) properties of the semiconductor.
1-018	Here, we have measured the transient photoinduced conductivity in the subnanosecond time range, for mesoporous TiO2 films with and without dye sensitization.
1-019	These measurements enable direct assessment of the charge injection rate, mobility, recombination, and trapping on this time scale.
1-020	The effect of treating TiO2 films with TiCl4 on charge injection and trapping rates has also been probed, with the intention of identifying the mechanisms that result in increased photocurrent and efficiency in TiCl4-treated DSCs.
1-021	Information obtained from studying photoconductivity dynamics can be crucial in answering questions about the efficacy of different dye sensitizers and of various surface treatments in improving charge injection and electron diffusion lengths in the DSCs and, therefore, provides valuable input for the optimization of electron transport and device performance.

2-003	After cleaning the substrates with acetone and IPA, an ∼100 nm thick compact layer of TiO2 was deposited at 450 °C using aerosol spray pyrolysis.[29]

2-005	To under- stand the effect of TiCl4 treatment on TiO2-based DSCs, some of the TiO 2 films were soaked in a 20 mM aqueous solution of TiCl4 for 4 h at room temperature in the dark.
2-006	After rinsing with DI water and drying in air, the films were then resintered to 500 °C for 45 min.

2-010	The alkyl chains are intended to help form a more compact monolayer of the dye on the TiO2 surface and also act as a blocking layer between the hole conductor and TiO2, thus resulting in faster charge injection and reduced recombination.[31,32]

2-018	Optical Pump Terahertz Probe (OPTP) Spectroscopy.
2-019	Photoconductivity transients were measured using an OPTP setup similar to that described in ref 27.
2-020	Briefly, 800 nm wavelength pulses of ∼40 fs duration originating from a Spectra- Physics Spitfire amplifier are used to generate either 400 nm pulses through frequency-doubling with a BBO crystal or 475-700 nm pulses by pumping an optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS, Spectra-Physics).
2-021	Photoexciting the sample with these pulses results in electrons being promoted into the TiO2 conduction band, where they are mobile and can interact with the THz probe pulse, incident on the sample at a time τ after the excitation pulse.
2-022	The change in amplitude and phase of the THz probe pulse transmitted through the photoexcited sample is measured using a double lock-in technique[23] and gives a direct measure of the photoconductivity of the sample material in the 0.2-2.0 THz frequency range.

3-030	The carrier mobility values extracted from our measurements can be compared with those previously determined by others for mesoporous TiO2 films.
3-031	Hendry et al. reported a mobility value of 0.01 cm 2/(V s) measured using a THz pump-probe technique similar to the one used in our study.[15]
3-032	However, Hendry’s study was based on a TiO2 paste made using Degussa P25 nanoparticles that are a mixture of 80% anatase and 20% rutile TiO 2, unlike the 100% anatase TiO2 paste used in this study (as determined by XRD).
3-033	On the other hand, a much higher mobility value of 15 cm2/(V s) was reported by Turner et al. also for a mixture of anatase and rutile TiO 2 nanopar- ticles,[13] using data analysis based on the Drude-Smith model.[37]
3-034	In addition to the standard Drude model that describes a free electron gas undergoing random elastic scattering events, Turner et al. have introduced an extra factor attributed to 90% backscattering from the grain boundaries and nanoparticle surfaces.
3-035	As a result, the modified Drude model yields higher
3-036	scattering time values as compared with estimates from other models, such as the effective medium theory.[15]
3-037	This results in the extracted mobility value being higher by a couple of orders of magnitude.
3-038	In contrast, in our study presented here, the carrier mobility has been directly extracted from experimentally measurable parameters, without employing any data-fitting models, and is, hence, the most direct method for estimating the true ac mobility in mesoporous TiO2.
3-039	Time-resolved microwave conductivity (TRMC) experiments at 9 GHz probe frequency have yielded carrier mobility values similar to the ones we find, ranging between 0.034 cm2/(V s) (using 9 nm anatase TiO2 nanoparticles purchased from So- laronix[16]) and 0.5 cm2/(V s) (thin anatase TiO2 film deposited using electron beam evaporation[17]).
3-040	However, much lower mobility values of ∼1 × 10-3 cm2/(V s) and ∼7 × 10-6 cm2/(V s) have been reported with transient and quasi-steady-state photocurrent experiments[20,21] and time-of-flight (TOF) measure- ments,[22] respectively, carried out at room-temperature.
3-041	This difference is not unexpected because the latter experimental techniques probe electron motion over a longer time scale (microsecond to second time range) corresponding to charge transport over longer distances for which initially mobile electrons have already experienced multiple trapping and detrapping events.

3-084	Effect of TiCl4 Treatment.
3-085	Several research groups have previously reported consistently improved device performance when an extra layer of TiO2 is grown on the surface of the sintered TiO2 nanoparticles by treating the films with TiCl4.[30,42–45]
3-086	However, the mechanisms underlying this improvement are still poorly understood.
3-087	In this section, we investigate the impact of such TiCl4 treatment on the early-time (subnanosecond) charge carrier injection and mobility in the TiO 2 mesoporous film.
3-088	To confirm the impact of TiCl4 treatment on device perfor- mance, solid-state DSCs were fabricated with and without the surface treatment of the sintered TiO2 film with TiCl4, as described in the Experimental Section.
3-089	As expected, the resulting photocurrent-voltage characteristics of the devices (shown in Figure 6) demonstrate a dramatic improvement in short-circuit current and efficiency with TiCl4 treatment.
3-090	As discussed in detail by Sommeling et al.,[43] this improve- ment could be a result of increased light harvesting, higher charge injection efficiency, and/or increased collection ef- ficiency, which, in turn, is a function of charge transport and recombination times.
3-091	Increased particle necking between the TiO2 nanoparticles caused by the TiCl4 treatment has also been suggested as a reason for the improved charge transport.[30,45]
3-092	Transient photocurrent measurements, however, have revealed only a marginal improvement in charge transport.[43,44]
3-093	Decreased recombination and increased dye loading have also been observed by Sommeling et al., but they noted that the changes were not enough to explain the increased photocurrent.
3-094	In agreement with their conclusion, we, in fact, obtain an enhance- ment in device performance with TiCl4 treatment even though we consistently observe a decrease in dye absorption (see Figure 1c) because of the different preparation protocol we used.
3-095	In their conclusion, Sommeling et al. have proposed that a positive shift in the TiO2 conduction band could lead to increased charge
3-096	injection and that this may be the main reason for the enhanced device performance.
3-097	We have employed OPTP to probe the charge dynamics in TiO2 films treated with TiCl4, in order to investigate if the charge injection rate, carrier mobility, or trapping rate in the picosecond to nanosecond time range are affected as a consequence of the treatment.
3-098	Figure 4 shows the resulting early- and later-time photoconductivity dynamics for dye-sensitized TiO2 films with TiCl4 treatment in comparison to as-sintered dyed TiO2 films for a similar absorbed photon fluence.
3-099	Contrary to expectation, we do not observe an increased charge injection rate as a result of the treatment, even when the samples are photoexcited at a range of wavelengths.
3-100	We also find that TiCl4 treatment appears to have little effect on the early-time carrier mobility.
3-101	Figure 2 shows the effective mobility extracted from undyed TiO 2 films treated with TiCl4 after excitation at 400 nm.
3-102	Values of φinjμpeak of ∼0.06 cm2/(V s) are observed, similar to those for samples without TiCl4 treatment.
3-103	In addition, the effective mobility was again determined for excitation of dyed samples across the absorption spectrum of the dye, as shown in Figure 5a, for both TiCl4-treated and untreated films.
3-104	We find no discernible differences between the two samples, suggesting that φinj is also largely unaffected by the TiCl4 treatment.
3-105	Finally, we have examined the effect of the treatment on the photoconductivity decay rate.
3-106	Figure 4b shows identical decay dynamics for samples with and without TiCl 4 treatment, whereas Figure 5b demonstrates that the extracted decay times are identical for the two cases across the whole dye absorption spectrum.
3-107	These observations demonstrate that the treatment does not affect carrier trapping at early times (up to a nanosecond), and thus, appears to have no significant effect on the density of sub-band-gap states in TiO2.
3-108	While these measurements are unable to yield the cause of the DSC performance enhancement with TiCl4 treatment, they allow us to eliminate a number of possible scenarios.
3-109	In particular, we clearly show that the TiCl4 treatment has no effect on the pico- to nanosecond charge-injection, decay, and mobility dynamics in dye-sensitized TiO2 .
3-110	These findings suggest that the origin of the increased photocurrents with TiCl4 treatment may be found by investigating more closely the processes occurring on longer time scales (nanosecond to millisecond), such as hole transfer, charge separation, and longer time recombination processes.
3-111	Finally, as a caveat to our findings, we note that careful spectroscopic analysis of complete devices under working conditions may yield information not attainable by studying dissected components, such as the dye-sensitized TiO2 probed here.
4-001	We have investigated the photoconductivity dynamics in mesoporous TiO2 films using an optical-pump terahertz-probe technique.
4-002	We find an intrinsic electron mobility value of ∼0.1 cm2/(V s) for both a homemade (EPFL) and a commercial (Dyesol) paste based on anatase TiO2 nanoparticles.
4-003	For TiO2 films sensitized with Z907 dye, biphasic charge injection is observed, with an initial sub-500 fs fast component, followed by a slower 70-200 ps rise component.
4-004	This is followed by a decay in photoconductivity over the first few nanoseconds, which appears to be caused by carrier trapping.
4-005	In addition, we have probed the effect of TiCl 4 treatment of sintered TiO 2 in order to identify reasons for improved device performance seen in TiCl 4-treated DSCs.
4-006	Repeated measurements on multiple samples show that the carrier mobility, charge injection ef- ficiency, and charge trapping times are not affected by the treatment with TiCl4 and are independent of the photoexcitation
4-007	wavelength.
4-008	A thorough understanding of such charge generation and transport mechanisms can help us to identify the bottlenecks and, hence, to design better materials and material combinations for optimized photovoltaic device performance.
