1-001	The oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) is of prime importance in view of its practical applications in green energy conversion/ storage systems ranging from fuel cells to metal/air batteries.[1-5] At present, materials containing precious metals such as platinum, palladium, and gold are the most successful electro- catalysts for the ORR.[3,6-9] The high costs and limited availability of the requisite raw materials have retarded large-scale com- mercial applications.
1-002	This has led to an urgent need for electrocatalysts made of inexpensive and more abundant materi- als but with comparable activity to precious metal based electrocatalysts.
1-003	Indeed, numerous nonprecious materials, such as carbon, polymer, transitional metals, and oxides, have been suggested as alternative electrocatalysts for the ORR.[1,2,4,10,11]
1-004	Among these materials, manganese oxides have drawn particular attention because of their low cost, high activity in alkaline media, and nontoxicity.[12-20] For example, Bruce and co-workers recently reported a successful prototype of a rechargeable Li/ O2 battery using an R-MnO2 nanowires catalyst, which can deliver a much higher capacity than conventional rechargeable lithium batteries.[21]
1-005	Additionally, Zhuang and co-workers have developed a high-performance OH- conductive polymer and used it to build an efficient prototype of a nonprecious metal alkaline polymer electrolyte fuel cell (APEFC). 22
1-006	It is generally observed that nanostructured materials exhibit favorable enhanced kinetics and activity derived from their large surface area and size effects.[23-25] Thus, in addition to chemical composition, a variety of factors, including the crystal phases present, structure, morphology, oxidation state, and surface area,
1-007	will remarkably impact physicochemical properties of these catalysts.[26-29] Nanostructured manganese oxides are promising low-cost materials for electrocatalysts for the ORR, but their widespread implementation hinges upon development of catalyst systems with high activity.
1-008	In addition, structure-activity relationships for nanostructured MnO2 for the ORR are required to elucidate the factors that determine catalyst performance.
1-009	The main challenge for such studies comes from the limited number of synthetic routes for controlled synthesis of MnO2 nanostructures.
1-010	These challenges have stimulated broad-based research efforts by many groups worldwide devoted to the synthesis of nanostructured manganese oxides.[30-35] Herein, we report a facile one-pot hydrothermal approach that permits exquisite control over the crystal structure and morphology of MnO2 nanostructures.
1-011	Specifically, simply varying the reaction temperature in our hydrothermal process, enables synthesis of three different MnO2 nanostructures (microsphere/nanosheet core-corona hierarchical architecture, one-dimensional nanorod, and nanotube) with controlled crystal phases and morphologies.
1-012	The formation mechanisms for each of these products are rationalized.
1-013	The as-prepared MnO2 nanostructures are found to possess excellent electrocatalytic activity in alkaline media for the apparent four-electron reduction of oxygen.
1-014	The vast difference in electroactivity from different products clearly attests to the importance of engineering the morphology, crystal phase and tunnel structure of MnO 2 nanostructures.

3-001	To study the growth mechanism of nano-MnO2, experiments are carried out at different temperatures for the same duration (12 h).
3-002	From the XRD pattern in Figure 1a, the product prepared at 100 °C is identified to be pure layered birnessite-type MnO2 (JCPDS No. 80-1098, monoclinic, C2/m, a ) 5.15 Å, b ) 2.84 Å, c ) 7.17 Å).
3-003	When the temperature is increased to 120 °C or higher, the products are found to be purely R-MnO2 (JCPDS No. 44-0141, tetragonal, I4/m, a ) b ) 9.78 Å, c ) 2.86 Å).
3-004	In general, MnO2 can form many polymorphs by interlinking the basic MnO6 octahedra in different configurations.
3-005	Figure 1b shows the schematic crystal structures of the two types of MnO2 .[36]
3-006	Birnessite-type MnO2, also denoted as δ-MnO2 in the literature, has a two-dimensional (2D) lamellar structure with an interlayer distance of 0.73 nm.
3-007	Structurally, R-MnO2 is constructed from the double chains of edge-sharing MnO6 octahedra which are linked at the corners to form (2 × 2) + (1 × 1) tunnel structures (the sizes of the 2 × 2 and 1 × 1 tunnels are 0.46 and 0.189 nm, respectively).
3-008	These tunnels extend in a direction parallel to the c axis of the tetragonal unit cell.
3-009	Since these structures possess either interlayer space or tunnels, some cations could be introduced during the synthesis process (the 1 × 1 tunnels are generally too small for cations).
3-010	Generally,
3-011	layered birnessite-type MnO2 needs more cations to stabilize the structure than tunneled R-MnO2 since the spacing in the birnessite is larger than that in R-MnO2 , which is confirmed by EDX analysis (see Supporting Information, Figure S1).
3-012	Parts a and b of Figure 2 show the SEM images of the product prepared at 100 °C. It can be observed that this product consists of microsphere/nanosheet hierarchical nanostructures.
3-013	The SEM image (Figure 2b inset) of a broken microsphere clearly reveals a core-corona architecture of the microsphere, in which the core is denser than the corona, and the nanosheets in the corona are grown perpendicularly from the core.
3-014	Parts a and b of Figure 3 show TEM images and a SAED patterns (Figure 3a inset) for the birnessite-MnO2.
3-015	The SAED pattern of a typical microsphere (Figure 3a inset) indicates the microsphere is polycrystalline.
3-016	The darker/lighter contrast again suggests the core-corona structure of the sphere.
3-017	Figure 3b shows a typical lattice-resolved TEM image of the nanosheet, indicating an average d-spacing of 0.6 nm.
3-018	This distance is lower than the interlayer space of perfect birnessite (0.73 nm) because birnessite tends to be dehydrated under the electron beam irradiation, which decreases the (001) d spacing.[30]
3-019	On the basis of our observations, the formation mechanism of the core-corona nanostructure might be rationalized as follows.[30-32] At the initial stage, the high concentrations of precursors lead to rapid formation of a large number of nuclei in a short time.
3-020	These nuclei self-assemble to form nearly amorphous spheres.
3-021	Afterward, the heterogeneous growth of 2D nanosheets on the dense cores is determined by the intrinsic layered crystal structure and should follow a general Ostwald
3-022	ripening process.
3-023	The as-formed microsphere/nanosheet core-corona hierarchical birnessite-MnO2 nanoarchitecture with a relatively high Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area
3-024	of about 63 m2 g-1 might exhibit fast electrode kinetics and good stability, making it promising as an electrode material for electrochemical energy conversion/storage systems.[23]
3-025	When the reaction temperature is increased to 120 °C, the product is tetragonal R-MnO2 (Figure 1a).
3-026	As a result, the morphology changes to tetragonal nanorods, as depicted in the SEM images (parts c and d of Figure 2).
3-027	The as-synthesized R-MnO 2 nanorods, typically 2-3 μm in length, have a square cross-section with an edge length in the range of 50-100 nm.
3-028	The BET surface area of these nanorods is measured to be about 19 m2 g-1.
3-029	Figure 3c displays a TEM image of a typical nanorod indicating a smooth surface, and a SAED pattern (inset) based on a single nanorod indicating the single-crystalline nature.
3-030	An average d spacing of about 0.5 nm is observed from the lattice- resolved TEM image (Figure 3d).
3-031	This d-spacing is consistent with the spacing between (200) planes.
3-032	The SAED pattern and HRTEM analysis suggest that the nanorods grow along the [001] direction (c axis).
3-033	In general, a higher temperature is favorable for anisotropic growth of crystal which gives higher aspect ratios.[33]
3-034	The rolling-cum-phase transformation process can be employed to depict the formation mechanism of the R-MnO2 nanorods.[33]
3-035	Specifically, during the initial stage of reaction, the relatively high K+ concentration facilitates the formation of 2D birnessite-MnO2 nanosheets.
3-036	The lamellar nanosheets tend to curl at an elevated temperature because the layered birnessite-MnO2 is a metastable phase.
3-037	Eventually, the layered structure collapses into 2 × 2 tunnel structure of R-MnO2, and the dissolution-crystallization process increases the size and aspect ratio of the final product.
3-038	Although R-MnO2 is also formed at 140 °C (Figure 1a), the morphology transforms to 1D nanotubes as shown in parts e and f of Figure 2 and Figure 3e.
3-039	The BET surface area of these nanotubes is about 25 m2 g-1.
3-040	Figure 2f reveals that each nanotube has a square open end.
3-041	HRTEM (Figure 3f) and SAED analysis (Figure 3e inset) suggest that the nanotubes possess the same growth direction as the nanorods prepared at 120 °C. On the basis of the above observations, the formation process of the nanotubes can be deduced as “dissolution or etching” of each nanorod from both ends inward along the long axis ([001] direction in this case), which leads to the formation of hollow tubes.[34]
3-042	A similar process has been also observed in the formation of ZnO nanorings and R-Fe2O3 nanotubes.[37,38]
3-043	In general, a higher temperature favors the occurrence of the dissolution or etching process. 37,38 Therefore, nanorods are formed at 120 °C, while all the products prepared at 140, 160, 180, and 200 °C are R-MnO2 nanotubes (see Figures S2 and S3 of Supporting Information).
3-044	Time-dependent experiments are also carried out at 140 °C in order to further understand the growth process (see Figures S4 and S5 of Supporting Information).
3-045	After 1 h of hydrothermal reaction, the product consists predominantly of birnessite-MnO2 with a microsphere/nanosheet hierarchical architecture.
3-046	With increasing duration, the anisotropic growth facilitates the formation of R-MnO2 nanorods.
3-047	Then the dissolution process occurs from the tip toward the center along the [001] direction.
3-048	Longer duration is required to form completely hollow R-MnO2 nanotubes.
3-049	The above observations are consistent with the recent findings.[34,35]

3-106	Previous studies have confirmed that the overall 4-electron pathway of the ORR (reaction 5-7) in MnO2 initiates from the electroreduction of MnO2 to MnOOH (reaction 5).[17-19] This reduction is unfavorable in MnO 2 phases which have a high Mn-O bond strength since it makes the initial reduction more difficult.
3-107	The average Mn-O bond lengths of birnessite-MnO2 and R-MnO2 are about 1.94 and 1.98 Å, respectively.[29]
3-108	Therefore, birnessite-MnO2 shows a higher Mn-O bond strength, which eventually results in a lower electrocatalytic activity.
3-109	It is also speculated that crystal phase (channel structure) could be another determining factor for electrocatalytic activity.
3-110	Generally, the electrocatalytic activity is in part determined by the reversible adsorption/desorption of reactants/products into and out of the open channel structure.
3-111	The large interlayer spacing (0.7 nm) in birnessite-MnO2 enables adsorp- tion of more O2 molecules, but the MnO6 octahedra sheet of birnessite-MnO2 is much denser than that of R-MnO2.
3-112	Overall the reducibility of birnessite-MnO2 is lower than that of R-MnO 2.[29]
3-113	Therefore, the ORR reaction in birnessite-MnO2 could be hindered by the excessively strong adsorption of reactant.
3-114	The pronounced difference in electrocatalytic activity between R-MnO2 and birnessite-MnO2 is further speculated from the different adsorption orientations of molecular oxygen in the two phases.[42,43]
3-115	The O-O bond length in molecular oxygen is about 0.12 nm which is comparable to the size of (1 × 1) tunnels in the R-MnO2.[44]
3-116	Hence the adsorbed oxygen could be accom- modated in the (1 × 1) channels via the bridge mode.
3-117	Since the bridge-mode adsorption (also known as the Yeager Model) is usually believed to favor the breaking of O-O bond of molecular oxygen and thus a direct 4-electron reduction of oxygen,[43-45] the R-MnO2 with (1 × 1) tunnels can therefore provide enhanced kinetics for oxygen reduction.
3-118	The anomalous difference in electroactivity may also partly originate from the intrinsic difference of activity on different crystal facets.
3-119	For example, birnessite-MnO 2 nanosheets and tetragonal R-MnO 2 nanorods/tubes have a large amount of exposed (001) and (200) surface, respectively.
3-120	Clearly further
3-121	investigation in this direction is required, ideally combined with computational calculations.
3-122	Although MnO2 nanostructures reported here are still inferior to precious metals as electrocata- lyst for the ORR, our findings suggest that engineering the shape, crystal phase, and microstructure could be an effective approach for enhancing the activity of nonprecious electrocatalyst for the ORR.
3-123	Our preliminary results show that further improvement of electrocatalytic activity can be achieved by making MnO2-based nanocomposites.
4-001	In summary, we report a facile one-pot hydrothermal route for synthesis of three MnO2 nanostructures and their self-assembled vertical arrays on substrates.
4-002	By simply adjusting the hydrothermal reaction temperature, we can tailor the morphology and crystal phase from birnessite-type MnO2 with a microsphere/nanosheet core-corona hierarchical architecture to R-MnO2 with 1D nanorod or nanotube structures.
4-003	Importantly, for the first time, we report a new finding that there is a strong dependence of the electroactivity of MnO2 for the ORR on its crystal phase.
4-004	The R-MnO2 exhibits superior ORR electroactivity compared to birnessite-type MnO2.This finding adds to our understanding of MnO2-based catalysts and further development of low-cost and high-efficiency alkaline polymer electrolyte fuel cells and metal-air batteries.
